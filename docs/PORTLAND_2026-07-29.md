# Portland zenith, 2026-07-29 — what the data says

**Dataset:** `Portland zenith.zip`, six zenith fields reduced under v1.3.6.
**Date of this note:** 2026-08-10. **Purpose:** a dress rehearsal for Leon, 2026-08-12.

Everything below was measured from the run's own output — `batch_summary.json`,
`activity.jsonl`, and the twelve archives. No raw frames were available, and that limits
two of the questions; where it does, it says so.

---

## 0. The run in one paragraph

Six fields, thirty frames each, captured between 02:09:54 and 02:22:53 with 153–165 s
between field starts. A 2×3 grid: header pointings at RA 307.68° / 313.92° and Dec
42.07° / 45.07° / 48.07°, so the fields do **not** overlap (grid step 4.4° × 3.0° against a
3.83° × 2.56° field of view). Sensor 6248 × 4176 at 2.2098″/px, which is an IMX571 body at
about 351 mm of focal length. All six plate-solved on the first attempt. Global rms
195–240 mas.

## 1. Did the software do what it should?

**Yes, and the fixes from last week are visible in the output.**

- **The header date was used.** Every field carries `observation_date 2026-07-29`,
  `date_guessed? false`, `date_guess_error_days null`. This is I0 working in folder mode —
  the London set of 2026-08-06 guessed sixteen different epochs spanning six months
  (ROADMAP §1.3).
- **Provenance is there.** `effective_options` (81 keys), `calibration`, `source_folder`,
  `alignment.shifts_px`, and the solve block are all in `results.txt` — I3, I6 and I16.
  The per-frame shifts in particular are what §3 below is built from; a week ago they
  went to a `print()`.
- **The solves were easy.** `n_clusters_checked: 1`, `anchor_rounds_used: 1`,
  `noise_px_used: 0.3` (the default, not escalated), 97–100+ stars matched against a
  threshold of 14. Nothing like the marginal ladder of ROADMAP §1.9.
- **The dark-free hot-pixel search had what it needs.** No darks or flats were taken, so
  it fell back to the dither — and the dither here is 55–68 px, far above the 3 px floor.
  It found 37–45 hot pixels per field.

Three things worth flagging, none of them failures:

- **Only one pattern-database layer is installed** (`patdb_g13_t17k`) on the reducing
  machine — the same single-layer state that cost two fields in the London set. It was
  not tested here, because nothing was marginal. The pre-flight check in ROADMAP §1.9
  still wants doing before the eclipse.
- **The double-star search was capped at G<15 by the catalogue** in all six fields, having
  asked for 17.0. `remove_double_tab2` is on, so unflagged close companions stayed in the
  fit. On a 2.2″/px scale with a 5.9″ FWHM a blend is an astrometric error, not a
  detection.
- **`enable_corrections` is False.** It costs nothing here — see §7 — but it will not be
  optional at Leon.

(Z1; the other five are the same shape.) Two things follow. First, **bright stars are
worse than faint ones at every radius** — so this is not photon noise, which would run the
other way, and the faint-end floor of 81–118 mas at V = 11–12 is about where a
photon-limited estimate from the detection SNR lands. Second, the growth is **radial**:
splitting the residual into radial and tangential components gives 254 vs 153 mas in the
outer band against 79 vs 81 in the inner one.

**It is not the distortion order.** Refitting Z1's own centroids at three orders:

| order | rms | nn_corr | 0–35% | 35–60% | 60–80% | 80–100% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| cubic | 195.0 | +0.121 | 70 | 101 | 133 | 202 |
| quintic | 188.3 | +0.044 | 67 | 94 | 125 | 185 |
| septic | 186.8 | +0.036 | 67 | 94 | 121 | 198 |

Quintic is worth having — `nn_corr` falls from 0.121 to 0.044, which says the cubic really

radial mean residual is within ±18 mas of zero in every band at every order, so there is no
under-fitted radial term left to find. **What grows at the edge is the star images
themselves** (§5), and no polynomial in position can fix a PSF that varies with position.

## 3. Polar alignment: off by degrees, and in azimuth

Every frame's alignment shift is now on disk, so this is measured rather than inferred.
Mapping the shifts through a pixel→sky Jacobian fitted from each field's own matched-star
table (which sidesteps every roll and mirror convention; the recovered scale agrees with
the solver's to 0.1% and the map is orthogonal to 2×10⁻³):

| field | dRA·cosδ | dDec | (arcsec per frame) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z1_base | +0.039 | −4.281 | |
| Z2_mid_left | +0.827 | −4.154 | |
| Z3_top_left | +1.939 | −4.493 | |
| Z4_top_right | +0.797 | −4.348 | |
| Z5_mid_right | +1.480 | −4.403 | |
| Z6_bottom_right | −0.075 | −4.388 | |

**97% of the drift is in declination.** A tracking-rate error produces declination drift of
exactly zero, so this is a polar-axis error and not a rate error — that alternative is
excluded, not merely disfavoured.

The frame cadence is not in the data (`read_observation_date` reads frame 0 only — F5/F7).
The first draft could only bound it: thirty frames plus a slew fit between consecutive
folder timestamps, the shortest gap being 153 s, so cadence ≤ 5.28 s and drift ≥ 51″/min,
giving 3.2° as a floor and 3–4° as the likely range.

**The capture script settles it.** `leon_zenith_v1.11.scs` is the file that took this data —
same six target names, 30 frames each, and its opening `MOUNT GOTO ALTAZ 80 265` puts Z1 at
the altitude Z1 actually solved at (80.1°, once the folder times are read as PDT). Its
exposure is **4 s**. With 153 s between field starts, a 10 s `DELAY`, SharpCap's 5 s
hardware settling and a short slew, the cadence is **4.5–4.7 s**, and

  **drift 4.48″/frame ÷ 4.6 s = 0.97″/s = 58″/min → ε = 0.97/15.041 = 0.065 rad = 3.7°**

using ω·ε = 15.041″/s × ε, the same relation that gave London its 0.5′ (ROADMAP §1.4).
So: **3.7°, with 3.2° as a hard floor.**

**It is an azimuth error, not an altitude one.** The fields sit at hour angles of **+8.6° to
+14.3°** — within an hour of the meridian — and declination drift near the meridian is the
textbook signature of azimuth error; altitude error shows itself in declination only well
away from it. Solving for the pole-offset direction puts it within **3.2°** of the
pure-azimuth axis, so the altitude component is under 5% of the total — under 10′ — while
the bearing is out by nearly four degrees. That is what aiming a mount by compass or by eye
produces; the magnetic declination at Portland, Oregon is about 15° east. (Which way round
in azimuth depends on a sign convention this dataset cannot settle; the magnitude does not.)

For scale: this is 450× the London mount's 0.5′, and it means the polar term now dominates
everything the mount does. **At 4 s it trails each zenith frame by 1.8 px**, and at the
6 s the horizon script uses, 2.6 px.

## 4. Periodic error: present, and ordinary

The drift is very nearly a straight line. Its departure from one, per field, after removing
the two discrete jumps found in §4.1:

| field | rms | peak-to-peak |
|---|---|---|
| Z1_base | 0.53″ | 2.40″ |
| Z2_mid_left | 0.61″ | 2.27″ |
| Z3_top_left | 0.67″ | 2.70″ |
| Z4_top_right | 0.75″ | 2.31″ |
| Z5_mid_right | 0.92″ | 3.03″ |
| Z6_bottom_right | 1.00″ | 3.65″ |

And the drift *rate* varies between fields: 4.281, 4.239, 4.756, 4.423, 4.653, 4.546 arcsec
per frame — **4.6% scatter** about the mean, over a thirteen-minute window that spans
one to two worm cycles.

Two independent readings of the same thing. The rate scatter implies a rate modulation of
0.055–0.065″/s, which for a worm period in the HEQ5/EQ6 range of 480–640 s is an amplitude
of **±4 to ±7 arcsec**. The within-field curvature says the same: a ±5″ sinusoid at 540 s,
sampled over thirty frames at 4.5 s, leaves 0.43″ rms about a straight line, against the
0.53–1.00″ observed.

**So yes, there is periodic error, at ±4–7″ — statistically the same mount behaviour as
London's ±7.7″.** The difference is that here it is 5% of the motion instead of nearly all
of it. Six rate samples over 1.5 cycles cannot pin the period; that would need per-frame
timestamps (F5) and a longer run.

### 4.1 Two discrete jumps, which are not periodic error

Robust outlier detection on the per-frame steps finds:

- **Z3, frame 28: a 7.59 px step** against a 2.14 px median (a 5.5 px excess, 30 MAD).
  This single event is why an earlier pass showed Z3 as four times less straight than the
  others; excluded, it is unremarkable. 7.6 px is 17 arcsec in one frame interval.
- **Z6, frame 12: 3.02 px** against 2.05 px median. Marginal, but the same shape.

A gust, a cable tug, or a slip. Worth knowing that they happen roughly once per 90 frames
on this setup, because during totality there is no second chance at the frame.

## 5. Focus: correctly focused, but the field is soft off axis

Per-star FWHM from the 1800+ stars measured in each field:

| | median | inner 35% | outer 25% | ratio | tilt in x | tilt in y | radial |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Z1 | 3.298 | 2.888 | 4.089 | 1.42 | −0.021 | −0.299 | +1.957 |
| Z2 | 3.310 | 2.914 | 4.112 | 1.41 | +0.002 | −0.303 | +1.911 |
| Z3 | 3.363 | 2.975 | 4.112 | 1.38 | +0.001 | −0.271 | +1.872 |
| Z4 | 3.432 | 2.958 | 4.169 | 1.41 | −0.008 | −0.290 | +1.911 |
| Z5 | 3.329 | 2.964 | 4.103 | 1.38 | +0.021 | −0.297 | +1.950 |
| Z6 | 3.470 | 2.984 | 4.226 | 1.42 | −0.023 | −0.298 | +2.012 |

(pixels; the tilt and radial columns are the fitted coefficients across the half-width,
half-height and half-diagonal respectively.)

Three readings, and they agree across all six fields to within a few percent:

1. **The focus itself is right.** The stacked PSF is cuspy — the azimuthal profile peaks at
   r = 0 and falls monotonically, 0.096 → 0.061 → 0.040 → 0.020 over the first two pixels.
   A defocused star from an unobstructed lens is flat-topped; this is the opposite shape.
   Half-power radius 1.25 px, so FWHM 2.50 px = 5.5″.
2. **The sensor is square to the optical axis.** The x-tilt coefficient is ±0.02 px, which
   is nothing; the y-tilt is −0.29 px across a half-height, small beside the radial term.
3. **The field is not flat.** The radial term is +1.9 to +2.0 px, and the corner is
   **1.4× the centre**. That is field curvature or an unmatched flattener spacing, not
   focus. This is what §2 is measuring at the field edge, and §5.1 says what it is.

**There is a lot of light outside the core.** 50% of the flux is inside r = 2.6″, but 80%
needs 6.0″ and 90% needs 8.6″. At r = 7 px the profile is 2.7×10⁻³ of the peak where a
Gaussian of the same FWHM would be 4×10⁻¹⁰. Those wings, and the fact that they grow and
turn asymmetric off axis, are the most likely reason bright stars centroid *worse* than
faint ones: the brighter the star, the further into the asymmetric wings the centroid
window reaches.

**Trailing is not the main problem.** The coherent (vector-mean) ellipticity across the
frame is 0.7–3.6%, aligned within 20–25° of the drift direction in the five fields where it
is measurable at all — so it is trailing, and it is small. The per-star median ellipticity
quoted in the summary (0.066–0.147) is a positive-definite quantity and is inflated by
noise; the vector mean is the honest one.

**Actionable:** check the backfocus spacing — §5.1. Recovering the corner to the centre's
2.9 px would take the outer-band residual from ~200 mas towards ~100 and the global rms
towards 140. That is a bigger win than anything in the software.

### 5.1 It looks like reducer spacing, and here is why

*Prompted by the question "if the 0.7× reducer is not spaced correctly, would it give this
kind of distortion?" The sensor is APS-C — 23.5 × 15.7 mm, 28.25 mm diagonal — so the
corner sits 14.1 mm off axis, which most 0.7× reducers are specified to cover.*

Pooling all six fields (10 900 stars) and binning by field radius:

| r / r_max | n | FWHM (px) | FWHM (″) | median \|e\| | e_radial | e_cross |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00–0.15 | 448 | 2.864 | 6.33 | 0.062 | −0.004 | −0.002 |
| 0.15–0.30 | 1362 | 2.950 | 6.52 | 0.060 | +0.004 | −0.001 |
| 0.30–0.45 | 2236 | 3.111 | 6.87 | 0.059 | −0.006 | −0.004 |
| 0.45–0.60 | 2844 | 3.375 | 7.46 | 0.058 | −0.010 | −0.009 |
| 0.60–0.75 | 2286 | 3.736 | 8.26 | 0.060 | −0.020 | −0.019 |
| 0.75–0.90 | 1568 | 4.109 | 9.08 | 0.063 | −0.042 | −0.015 |
| 0.90–1.05 | 171 | 4.361 | 9.64 | 0.075 | −0.064 | −0.010 |

Negative `e_radial` means the major axis lies *perpendicular* to the radius — tangential.

Four things the Seidel terms let us read off this, because they scale differently with
field height h:

1. **It is not coma.** Fitting FWHM² = c₀ + c₁·r + c₂·r² gives c₁ = −3.6 and c₂ = +17.1;
   an r²-only fit is as good as both terms together (residual 1.937 vs 1.930 px²). Coma
   blur grows as h¹. Astigmatism and field curvature grow as h². **The blur here is
   purely h².** Coma would also displace the centroid radially, and the mean radial
   residual is within ±18 mas of zero in every band (§2).
2. **It is not tilt or decentring.** The pattern is rotationally symmetric about the
   *sensor* centre: the fitted x-tilt is ±0.02 px, and the corner/centre FWHM ratio is
   1.357, 1.380, 1.387, 1.388, 1.391, 1.358 — the same in all six fields. A tilted or
   decentred element puts the sharp zone off centre and makes one side worse than the
   other. **The optical axis passes through the sensor centre, and there is exactly one
   number wrong: the axial spacing.**
3. **It is not the stack.** A residual rotation in a translation-only stack smears stars
   tangentially, ∝ r — a real effect here (§3, the alignment residual runs from 0.11 px in
   Z3 and Z5 to 0.62 px in Z1). But the corner/centre FWHM ratio is 1.36–1.39 whether the
   stack rotated a little or six times as much, and the arithmetic says why: even Z1's
   0.62 px of residual is only 1.16 px of corner smear, which takes a 2.86 px PSF to
   2.92 px. **It accounts for 4% of the 1.50 px growth.** It does contribute to the
   tangential ellipticity — but Z3 and Z5, with almost no stack rotation, still show
   −0.024 and −0.036 at the corner, so most of that is optical too.
4. **Focus was set on the centre.** A parabola through the binned FWHM has its minimum at
   r/r_max = −0.28, i.e. at or inside the centre, degrading monotonically outward. With a
   flat sensor in a residually curved field you can put *one* radius in focus; the centre
   was chosen, so the corner carries the whole sag.

**So: an image that grows as h², stays nearly round, picks up a small tangential
astigmatism, has no coma, and is symmetric about the sensor centre. That is residual field
curvature plus a little astigmatism — and spacing is the knob that sets both.** The answer
to the question is yes.

How much: the corner adds √(4.36² − 2.86²) = 3.29 px of blur in quadrature, which at
3.76 µm is 12.4 µm. At the reduced focal ratio (the FRA500 is 90 mm, so f/3.90 at 351 mm)
that is about **48 µm of defocus at the corner** — a best-focus surface sagging by 48 µm
over 14.1 mm of field radius, a curvature radius near 2.1 m.

**Which way to move it, this data cannot say.** The tangential orientation says which side
of the astigmatic medial focus the sensor sits on, but that maps to "too close" or "too
far" only through the design prescription. One test run settles it: change the spacer by
1 mm and see whether the corner improves.

### 5.2 The plate scale is a micron-resolution spacing gauge

Worth knowing before doing that test, because it makes it cheap and exact.

A reducer of factor *m* and focal length *f_r* sits a distance *d* in front of the
telescope's native focus, with *m* = f_r/(d + f_r) and a back focus of *m·d*. Move the
sensor by ΔB and refocus, and the new equilibrium has

  **Δm / m = −ΔB / (m · f_r)**

For a 0.7× reducer with a 55 mm design back focus, f_r ≈ 183 mm, so **1 mm of spacing moves
the plate scale by 0.78%** — 17 mas/px on this system.

This run measured the plate scale to **5 ppm** across six fields. That is 11 µas/px, or
**about 1 µm of spacing**. Nothing else in an amateur setup measures a mechanical distance
to a micron.

So the procedure is: take a short zenith set at each of three or four spacer thicknesses,
and for each one record the plate scale (which says exactly and repeatably where you were)
and the corner/centre FWHM ratio (which says how good it was). The ratio against spacing is
a curve with a minimum; the plate scale is a perfectly linear ruler for its x-axis. Three
fields of thirty frames each is under ten minutes per setting.

One tempting shortcut, and its limit: the measured focal length is **350.96 mm**. If the
native focal length were exactly 500 mm and the reducer exactly 0.700×, the design value
would be 350.0 and the +0.27% excess would say the sensor sits ~0.35 mm short of design.
But manufacturers' nominal figures are routinely 1% out in either the focal length or the
reduction factor, and 1% is 1.3 mm of spacing. **Use the differential method, which needs
no nominal numbers.** A well-spaced flattener on APS-C should get the corner/centre ratio
under about 1.1, against the 1.38 measured here.

### 5.3 What the centre softness is, and is not

The centre is 2.86 px = 6.3″ by moments, 2.50 px = 5.5″ by the stacked profile's half-power
radius. That is soft for a 90 mm aperture, whose diffraction FWHM is about 1.3″. It is
probably **not** the optics: the Moffat wing index fitted to the stacked PSF is 5.44, 4.06,
3.82, 2.84, 4.95, 4.44 — mean 4.3, against 4.765 for pure Kolmogorov seeing. Aberrations do
not produce that wing law; the atmosphere does.

The caveat is that a mount misaligned by 3–4° may also be jittering inside each 2–5 s
exposure at frequencies the frame-to-frame shifts cannot see, and that would broaden the
core in a broadly similar way. Separating the two needs a focus/exposure sweep, or simply
repeating the test once the mount is aligned.

## 7. Noise

The honest answer is that **this cannot be measured from the reduced output**, and the
reason is worth recording: no darks or flats were taken (`n_darks: 0, n_flats: 0`), and the
run does not harvest `EGAIN`, `RDNOISE`, `GAIN`, `OFFSET` or `CCD-TEMP` from the FITS
headers (F7 is unimplemented). So read noise, dark current and sensor temperature are all
absent from the record. For the London set those numbers came from fifty darks and fifty
flats, and they are how §1.8 of the roadmap exists at all.

What the data does support:

- The centroid residual bottoms out at **81–118 mas at V = 11–12** and rises at both ends.
  The faint side is about where a photon-limited estimate from the detection SNR lands, so
  the faint end behaves like a noise-limited measurement should.
- The bright end sits **five to ten times above** that floor, which is a systematic, and
  §5 says what kind.
- The dark-free defect search found only 37–45 hot pixels per field out of 19 000–37 000
  bright candidates. The London ASI533 darks found 13 290 defects above the 20σ cut. The
  dark-free path catches the worst offenders and nothing else — which is fine when the
  dither is 55–68 px and every defect lands on a different star each frame, and not fine at
  all on a stack that does not move.

**Take darks at Leon**, at every exposure of the ladder. Defect amplitude is
`pedestal + rate × t`, so tiers cannot be extrapolated from one another (ROADMAP §1.8), and
fifty frames at each of five exposures is about four minutes.

## 8. What this means for Leon, 2026-08-12

At Leon (42.60°N, 5.57°W) greatest eclipse is near **18:30 UT with the Sun at 9.5°
altitude**, azimuth 281°. *An earlier draft of this section said 16–18°, from the
geocentric Sun–Moon separation; lunar parallax at that hour is about a degree, and the
topocentric minimum falls forty-five minutes later and eleven degrees lower. Corrected by
Andrew.* Consequences, in order of how much they matter:

1. **Fix the polar alignment.** Not because of trailing — at ladder exposures the trailing
   from a 3.5° error is 0.04 px at 0.1 s and 0.9 px at 2.4 s, and only the longest rung
   suffers (2.7% broadening) — but because of two other things:
   - **The field walks 153″ = 69 px per 180 s.** Alignment removes it from the stack; it
     does not remove it from the *framing*, and the corona will not stay where it was put.
   - **Field rotation.** The same misalignment carries a residual rotation of up to
     0.85″/s, complementary to the drift and depending on where the mount points — at Leon,
     with the Sun in the west at low altitude, the split will be different from these
     zenith fields and may favour rotation. Up to 153″ over 180 s is **2.8 px of smear at
     the frame corner that a translation-only alignment cannot remove**. This makes F2
     (per-frame affine alignment) matter far more here than it did in London, where the
     whole term was 0.08 px.

   A twenty-minute SharpCap polar alignment takes 3.5° to under 1′ and removes all of this.

2. **Turn the refraction correction on, and give it a site and a time.** At 9.5° altitude
   the refraction itself is 5.7′, and across a 2.56° field (Bennett's formula, not the
   tan z approximation, which fails down there):

   | Sun altitude | refraction | linear across the field | quadratic |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 70° (this test) | 22″ | 3.1″ | 0.01″ |
   | 17° (the wrong number) | 3.2′ | 30″ | 1.0″ |
   | **9.5° (Leon)** | **5.7′** | **83″** | **4.7″** |

   The linear part disappears into the plate scale and the quadratic into the distortion
   polynomial, so the *rms will not show it* — but the distortion solution will be wrong by
   an amount that is degenerate with the deflection signal, and 4.7″ is 24× this test's
   whole rms. This needs `enable_corrections_ref`, `observation_lat/long` and
   `observation_time`, none of which the app window can set today (ROADMAP F9, Q1). **This
   is a blocker, not a convenience,** and the eleven-degree correction to the altitude
   turned it from a 1″ problem into a 5″ one.

2b. **Atmospheric dispersion, which is a separate problem and may be the larger one.**
   At 9.5° the refraction differs by **8.6″ between 400 and 700 nm** (3.9 px), or 5.2″
   across a 450–650 nm response (2.3 px). Unfiltered on a mono sensor, every star becomes a
   short vertical spectrum whose centroid depends on its colour, and the pipeline's
   refraction model takes a single `observation_wavelength` (0.65 µm by default), so it
   corrects the mean and not the spread. At the zenith this is 0.25 px and ignorable; at
   Leon it is not. A moderately narrow filter, or an atmospheric dispersion corrector, or
   both. Worth settling before the day rather than on it.

3. **Check the reducer spacing.** §5.1: the corner is 1.38× the centre, the signature is
   specifically a spacing error rather than tilt or coma, and it costs more rms than
   anything in the software. §5.2 has a method that uses the plate scale as a
   micron-resolution ruler, at ten minutes per setting.

4. **Take darks at every ladder exposure**, and flats. §7.

5. **Use quintic, not cubic.** Free, and `nn_corr` says the cubic is leaving structure.

6. **Build the full pattern-database layer set on the reducing machine** before the day,
   and confirm `layers_used` shows more than one.

7. **Dither.** The mount settles inside one frame (§6), and the drift already supplies
   55–68 px of accidental dither here — but a properly aligned mount will not, and 20–50 px
   of deliberate dither costs 1–2% of the field and no trailing at all.

## 9. Method notes

- The pixel→sky map was fitted from each field's own `CATALOGUE_MATCHED_ERRORS.csv` rather
  than reconstructed from `roll` and `mirror`. It is checked two ways: the recovered scale
  matches the solver's to 0.1%, and the map is orthogonal to 2×10⁻³.
- `shifts_px` is `(position in frame 0 − position in frame i)` in `(row, col)`, so the
  image moves by *minus* the recorded shift. The magnitude conclusions do not depend on
  this; the drift direction does.
- The azimuth-versus-altitude split is asserted from the classical drift-alignment rule
  (declination drift near the meridian is azimuth error) as well as from the fitted
  pole-offset direction, because the two do not share a sign convention and agreeing is
  worth more than either alone.
- The polar error and the periodic-error amplitude both scale with the frame cadence, which
  is not recorded. The first draft could only bracket it from the folder timestamps; the
  capture script (`leon_zenith_v1.11.scs`, 4 s exposures) then pinned it, and the numbers
  above are the pinned ones. That the answer had to come from outside the dataset is the
  argument for F5 and F7: **they would remove this caveat** — `JD_UTC` is in every frame header and the pipeline
  reads only frame 0's.
- The folder times are **PDT**, not UTC: `MOUNT GOTO ALTAZ 80 265` in the capture script
  reproduces Z1's solved altitude only at UTC−7. An earlier draft used UTC−8, which moved
  every hour angle by 15° and every altitude by 10°. Nothing in §3 depended on it — the
  polar fit is entirely in equatorial coordinates — but the meridian argument is stronger
  at the corrected hour angles, not weaker.
- Totality timing at Leon is from the **topocentric** Sun–Moon separation, with the Moon
  from `get_body('moon', t, location=...)`. The first draft used the geocentric separation
  and was wrong by 45 minutes and 11° of altitude, because the Moon's parallax is about a
  degree — which is the same size as the quantity being minimised. It is a trap worth
  recording: the geocentric curve has a clean minimum and looks perfectly convincing.
- Refraction figures use Bennett's formula, not `58.3″ · tan z`, which overestimates by
  about 15% at 9.5° altitude. Dispersion uses the two-term Ciddor dispersion of dry air.

## 12. What the raw frames settle (added 2026-08-20)

The note above was written from run output only, and said so: "No raw frames were available,
and that limits two of the questions." The frames are now at

### 12.1 The cadence is 4.39 s, measured

### 12.2 Static pointing: the declination offset *is* the polar error

Section 3 measured *drift* -- change within a field. This is the absolute offset between where
the mount thought it pointed (the `RA`/`DEC` header keys) and where the field solved, which is
a different measurement and the one that carries a cone-error signature.

| field | H | dec | dRA*cos(dec) | dDec |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Z1_base | +13.65 deg | 42.07 | +0.260 deg | **+1.301 deg** |
| Z2_mid_left | +14.29 | 45.07 | +0.522 | **+1.311** |
| Z3_top_left | +14.93 | 48.07 | +0.774 | **+1.329** |
| Z4_top_right | +9.37 | 48.07 | +0.840 | **+0.945** |
| Z5_mid_right | +10.01 | 45.07 | +0.565 | **+1.001** |
| Z6_bottom_right | +10.65 | 42.07 | +0.285 | **+1.065** |

**The error is not "on the RA axis".** Declination is the larger component throughout, and the
two components have different structure: dDec tracks *hour angle* (about 1.31 deg for the
H~14 deg column, about 1.00 for the H~10 deg column) while dRA*cos(dec) tracks *declination*
(+0.26 at dec 42 to +0.84 at dec 48, in both columns).

Fitting the standard TPOINT terms to all twelve measurements:

| term | value | note |
|---|---|---|
| **MA** polar azimuth | **+4.20 deg** | dropping it takes the residual from 51" to 409" |
| **ME** polar altitude | **-0.015 deg** | 54 arcsec: 0.4% of MA. Section 3's "under 10 arcmin" tightens to under 1 arcmin |
| ID dec index | +0.27 deg | |
| IH / CH / NP | degenerate | see 12.3 |

residual **51 arcsec**. And the declination offsets are simply `MA*sin(H) + ID` -- Z1:
4.204*sin(13.65 deg) + 0.274 = 1.266 deg against 1.301 measured. That is why dDec follows hour
angle: it *is* the azimuth error projected through H, not an independent declination fault.

A 51 arcsec residual is large against 0.2 arcsec astrometry, so the model is leaving real
structure. Expected: these terms are first-order in the misalignments and 4 degrees is not a
small angle. Do not read the coefficients past two significant figures.

### 12.3 Cone error cannot be measured from this dataset

Asked directly, because the mount's setting circles were reportedly a few degrees out. The
answer is that this data cannot separate a cone error from an hour-angle index error:

- with a cone term: `IH=+1.70, CH=+2.19, NP=-1.45` -> residual **51"**
- without one: `IH=+3.25, NP=+0.10` -> residual **51"**

Identical, with condition numbers of 3458 and 49. `CH` enters as a constant and `IH` as
`IH*cos(dec)`, and across dec 42-48 `cos(dec)` moves only from 0.743 to 0.669 -- ten percent of
leverage, which is not enough. A significant cone error is fully *consistent* with this data
and simply not *measurable* from it. Separating them needs fields spanning a wide declination
range, ideally dec 0 to 70+; six fields in a 6x6 degree box cannot, however good the
astrometry.

### 12.5 The stacking hypothesis, tested where it should show

This is the dataset the cross-analysis note's section 7 needed (see `ROADMAP.md` section 3a):
star-rich, and with the field rotation its mechanism requires actually present. Z5, quintic in
both runs, single frame against the 30-frame stack:

| | run | stars | rms | nn | 0-20' | 20-40' | 40-60' | 60-80' |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **radial** | 1 frame | 2403 | 276.7 mas | 0.149 | +24.6+-11.9 | **+40.8+-9.1** | +11.4+-7.8 | -24.6+-7.6 |
| | 30 frames | 2426 | 204.6 mas | **0.027** | +6.9+-10.8 | -13.8+-7.2 | +0.4+-5.5 | +8.6+-5.6 |
| **tangential** | 1 frame | 2403 | | | +3.1+-11.3 | -5.1+-9.2 | -2.3+-7.5 | -1.5+-6.6 |
| | 30 frames | 2426 | | | -6.3+-12.9 | -2.0+-6.0 | -0.5+-4.9 | +0.8+-4.5 |

Three things, and they point the same way:

1. **The single frame carries more radial structure than the stack**, not less -- a coherent
   +25/+41/+11/-25 mas wave, 4.5 sigma at its peak. The stack is flat to 1-2 sigma.
2. **Spatially correlated residual falls sharply on stacking**: nn_corr 0.149 -> 0.027.
3. **The tangential residual is null in both** (0-6 mas, all within about 1 sigma). That is
   where un-removed field rotation would appear, and it is not there.

**So the hypothesis is not supported on the dataset best able to test it.** Stacking improves
this field's astrometry rather than biasing it.

There is a physical reason, and it is worth writing down because it also bounds what F2 can
buy: a *uniform* field rotation is absorbed by the plate solve as a change in ROLL. What
survives translation-only alignment is the **smear** -- PSF elongation growing with radius --
which costs centroid *precision*, not accuracy, unless the smear is asymmetric. At 0.72 px at
the corner against a 2.67 px FWHM, that is a real but modest precision cost.

**F2 remains worth doing on section 1.5's evidence.** It should not be justified as fixing a
radial position bias, because on this data there is no such bias to fix.

## 13. The capture script, the hot pixels, and where the 200 mas comes from

The scripts that took this data are now in `I:\Toby Portland 2026\portland_2026-07-29.zip`.

### 13.1 The script names the cause of the pointing error

**Correction to section 3:** that section attributed this data to `leon_zenith_v1.11.scs`. The
file that actually took it is **`portland_zenith_v1.2.scs`**, and its header states the rig
outright: **FRA500 + 0.7x reducer (350 mm f/3.9), ASI2600MM Pro, Celestron AVX**. So this is
the Leon optical train -- and Don Bruns' recommended combination -- on an entry-level German
equatorial.

The mechanism is in the first mount command:

```
MOUNT GOTO ALTAZ 80 265        # Z1
MOUNT OFFSET 0 3               # Z2, Z3 -- relative, in the mount's own RA/Dec
MOUNT OFFSET 0.416 0           # Z4
```

The grid is established by **one alt-az slew** and then five **relative offsets in the mount's
own coordinates**. An alt-az goto forces the mount to convert altitude and azimuth into its
internal RA/Dec, using its own polar model -- so a 4 degree polar error puts the first field
somewhere other than where it was asked to go, and the header's `RA`/`DEC` faithfully record
the mount's *belief*, not the sky. Every later field inherits that, because the offsets are
exact in the mount's frame: `MOUNT OFFSET 0.416 0` is 6.24 degrees, and the header RA
difference between the columns is 6.24 degrees, to the digit.

That is why the pointing residuals in section 12.2 are so smoothly structured rather than
scattered: they are one misaligned frame viewed at six places, not six independent errors. It
also means the script's stated precondition -- "polar aligned" -- was not met, and an alt-az
goto is the command most sensitive to that.

**For the campaign:** an alt-az goto on a mount whose alignment is not trusted will always
land somewhere else. Either polar align properly first, or command RA/Dec so the error shows
up as a framing offset rather than propagating through the whole grid.

### 13.2 Hot pixels: removal worked, and the drift is what made it work

Asked because the declination drift is so large. The answer is that the drift is not a problem
here, it is the *enabling condition*.

**No darks were taken for Portland** -- there is no darks folder, and the script captures
none. So the dark-based defect mask could not run. The dark-free path did
(`hot_pixel_dark_free`, default on), and it works by exactly the distinction the drift
provides: a star is fixed to the sky, a hot pixel is fixed to the detector, and a dither tells
them apart (`bench/HOTPIX.md`). Re-reducing Z5 reports:

```
37 hot pixel(s) identified from the dither (61.1 px) out of 36823 bright candidates,
without a dark frame
2 centroid(s) dropped as hot pixels; realigning without them
```

The 61.1 px the search used **is** the polar drift measured in section 12.4 (135 arcsec at
2.2097 arcsec/px = 61 px). A deliberate dither is usually a few px; this is sixty. So the
misalignment that ruins the framing is simultaneously giving the dark-free defect search an
unusually strong signal, and a badly aligned mount is the *best* case for it, not the worst.

Two consequences worth keeping:

- **The sensor is clean**: 37 hot pixels out of 36 823 bright candidates, and only 2 centroids
  affected. Hot pixels are not a contributor to this field's error budget.
- **The large unmatched-centroid count is not defects.** The stack finds 12 604 centroids and
  matches 2 426; the excess is real stars fainter than the magnitude-13 catalogue cut, not
  hot-pixel trails, since only 37 candidates out of 36 823 were flagged.

### 13.3 The 200 mas is optical and radial, not rotational smear

The question was whether the un-removed field rotation elongates edge stars and inflates the
rms. It does not, and the residual *scatter* by radius says so cleanly -- rotational smear is
**tangential** (a rotation displaces a star at radius r tangentially by r*dtheta), so it would
show as tangential scatter growing with radius, in the stack only.

Standard deviation of the residual, mas, Z5, quintic:

| | | 0-20' | 20-40' | 40-60' | 60-80' | 80-100' | 100-140' |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 frame | radial | 110 | 143 | 160 | 188 | 234 | **291** |
| | tangential | 105 | 145 | 153 | 163 | 183 | **210** |
| 30 frames | radial | 100 | 115 | 113 | 141 | 178 | **242** |
| | tangential | 119 | 95 | 101 | 112 | 117 | **135** |

**The growth is radial, not tangential.** In the stack the tangential scatter is nearly flat
across the whole field (119 to 135 mas, +13%) while the radial scatter grows 2.4x (100 to
242). Rotation smear is therefore excluded: it would do the opposite.

Radial elongation growing with field radius is the signature of **coma or lateral colour**,
both of which a focal reducer introduces and both of which grow with radius. The FRA500 with a
0.7x reducer is the configuration Don's paper measures as adding cubic distortion; on this
evidence it is also degrading the edge PSF radially. Consistent with section 2's finding that
the field is not flat.

**Not seeing.** FWHM is 5.89 / 5.93 / 5.75 / 5.97 / 5.75 / 6.22 arcsec across the six fields
over thirteen minutes -- an 8% spread. Atmospheric seeing varies more than that on that
timescale. A stable FWHM with a strong *radial* field dependence is optics, not air.

**Focus may contribute but cannot be the whole story**, because defocus is symmetric: it would
raise radial and tangential scatter together, and the tangential term is flat. Worth noting
the script specifies "focused with red filter in train", so if the filter was out during
imaging the lateral-colour term would be worse.

**And the brightest stars are the worst measured, from saturation.** Scatter by magnitude
(total, mas):

| run | mag 6-9 | 9-10 | 10-11 | 11-12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 frame | **210** | 170 | 133 | 152 |
| 30 frames | **164** | 144 | 128 | 136 |

That is backwards for photon noise, which would put the faintest stars worst. Checking peak
pixel values against the frame directly: the frame maximum is exactly **65535**, the median
peak for magnitude 6-8 stars is 33 282, and **22% of them exceed 60 000 ADU** -- so a fifth of
the brightest stars are clipped at 4 s, and a clipped star's centroid is biased. Nothing
fainter than magnitude 8 gets near the ceiling.

**Why the distortion model still looks good.** It does: the *mean* radial residual after a
quintic fit is flat to 1-2 sigma (section 12.5). A polynomial corrects the systematic
displacement, and it does that well. What it cannot do is reduce the per-star *scatter* from an
elongated or clipped PSF -- that is a centroid problem, not a geometric one. The two facts are
consistent, and together they say the 200 mas is dominated by centroid quality at the field
edge rather than by anything the fit could absorb.

**So, in order of what to fix for the campaign:** polar alignment (framing, and it drives
everything in section 3), then exposure or a shorter rung for the brightest stars (saturation
at 4 s), then the edge PSF -- which for a fixed optical train means either accepting a smaller
usable field or checking focus and filter placement. The rotation is real but is not what is
costing the rms.
