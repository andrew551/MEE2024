# Leon, Spain — the 2026-08-12 eclipse campaign data

**Date:** 2026-08-17, against v1.3.7
**Data:** `I:\FRA500 Leon Aug 2026`
**Status:** the calibration half is measured and built. The refraction half is scoped, with
the numbers that say how much it matters, and not yet done.

This is the first campaign whose calibration data went through
[`mee2024/calibration.py`](../mee2024/calibration.py) — F1 in [`ROADMAP.md`](ROADMAP.md) —
and it is the dataset that feature was designed against. It is also the first dataset where
**vertical refraction, not distortion, is the dominant error term**, because the eclipse
happened 9.7° above the horizon.

`Eclipse/CAL_piLeo` is empty and `SCI_ladder` still holds duplicate `(2).fits` files, as
expected — pre-processing selection is still in progress.

### 2.1 `BIASADU` is trustworthy on this camera — unlike the 533MM

Header 500.91 (G0) and 501.14 (G101) against measured 502.40 and 502.60: **within 0.3%**.
On the London ASI533MM the same keyword was out by a factor of 4.16 (393.59 claimed, 94.66
measured), which is why the builder checks rather than trusts. Here it checks out, and the
check is what lets us say so.

### 2.2 Dark current is not measurable at this temperature

The G0 median rises 502.396 → 502.438 across the whole 0.1–2.0 s ladder: **0.042 ADU total**,
or about 0.03 e⁻ at `EGAIN` 0.777. The last three tiers are identical to three decimals, so
the ladder has run into the resolution of the measurement rather than into a trend.

Read that as an upper bound, not a rate: **dark current at +10 °C is below what a 26 MP
median can resolve over two seconds.** It is consistent with the capture script's stated
0.003 e⁻/px/s. The practical consequence is the one the script already assumed — for these
exposures the darks are a **defect map and a bias**, not a signal to subtract.

### 2.3 Read noise measures lower than the header claims

| gain | measured σ | × `EGAIN` | header `RDNOISE` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 3.35 ADU | 2.60 e⁻ | 3.49 e⁻ |
| 101 | 4.02 ADU | 1.05 e⁻ | 1.54 e⁻ |

Both about 25–30% below the header value, consistently across gains. `RDNOISE` is probably a
spec figure rather than a measurement of this body. **Worth resolving before it is used in an
error budget**, since a noise model that is 30% pessimistic makes every other term look
smaller than it is. The per-pixel σ maps are in the library beside each master if someone
wants to re-derive it.

### 2.4 The flat justifies taking no flat-darks — measured, not assumed

**Fill 50.7% of full scale** (33 223 of 65 535), which is where its capture script aimed
("histogram peaks mid-range, ~30–35k ADU") and inside the 25–75% band the no-flat-dark
argument needs. The unsubtracted offset pedestal is diluted by the signal, so at half scale
it leaves ~0.3% at a vignetted corner — smooth, multiplicative, centroid-irrelevant.

So for this campaign the decision to retire flat-darks is supported by the data rather than
by the argument alone. PRNU is 1.278%.

One flat set at gain 0 serves the gain-101 night data too, on the stated first-order
assumption that PRNU and vignetting do not depend on gain. The library applies it and
**says so** each time, rather than leaving the assumption implicit.

### 2.5 Why min-max rejection and not sigma-clipping

Worth recording, because sigma-clipping is the obvious first choice and it was tried first.

Clipping needs a spread to measure against, and the only spread a streaming pass has is the
per-pixel standard deviation — which the outlier itself inflates. One frame at 60 000 ADU
among forty-nine at 500 gives that pixel a σ near 17 800, so **even a 5σ cut keeps the
spike.** Verified as a test rather than reasoned about.

Dropping each pixel's own extreme high and low frame needs no scale estimate, comes free from
the same accumulators (`mean = (sum − min − max) / (n − 2)`), and discriminates on the right
axis: a cosmic ray is extreme in *one* frame, while a hot or telegraph pixel is high in
*every* frame and survives with 2 of 50 samples trimmed. It also halved the build time —
**582 s to 324 s** — because there is no second pass over 24 GB.

The σ map written beside each master is deliberately **untrimmed**: it is the only thing that
finds telegraph/RTS pixels, and trimming each pixel's extremes is exactly what would hide
them.

### 2.7 One warning had to be fixed to make this readable

The first end-to-end run printed *"the calibrated stack runs 66 ADU below zero... usually means
the darks do not match the lights"* — on a frame calibrated with the exactly-matching tier at
the same gain, exposure and setpoint.

The test was `if pedestal:`, i.e. *any* pixel below zero. On 26 million pixels with 4 ADU of
read noise the single lowest pixel sits five or six sigma under by construction, and dividing
by a vignetted flat deepens it, so that warning fired on **every correctly calibrated frame** —
the fastest way to teach someone to ignore a warning. It now reports the pedestal plainly
always, and warns only when more than 1% of the frame is negative, which is what a genuinely
mismatched dark looks like.

## 3. The site, exactly

**The FITS headers give a city, not a site — by design.** They carry `OBSLAT 42.5799722`,
`OBSLONG -5.57`, no altitude keyword at all, and `LOCATION 'Leon'`. That comes from the CPWI
mount software's built-in list of world locations, where "León" is an entry: **for mount
control, knowing which city you are in is sufficient**, and CPWI is not trying to answer an
astrometric question.

This matters for how F7 should treat it. The coordinates are not stale, not a
misconfiguration, and will not be "fixed" upstream — they are correct for their purpose and
wrong for ours. Any header harvest must treat mount-derived site coordinates as **city-level
by construction**, and needs somewhere else to record the real site.

The observing site was north of the city, and `actual leon site.JPG` records where:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude | 42° 44′ 25.69″ N | **+42.740470°** |
| Longitude | 5° 36′ 49.60″ W | **−5.613780°** |
| Altitude | 1101.0 m (3612 ft) | **1101 m** |
| ΔT | 69.1 s | |

The header is **17.8 km south and 3.6 km west** of that, and silent about 1101 m of
elevation. Use the site record, not the header. (The same trap as the Bruns 2017 eclipse
data, whose headers also carry the wrong site — so this is a pattern, not an accident, and
F7 must treat `OBSLAT`/`OBSLONG` as a hint to be checked rather than a fact.)

**The altitude is confirmed by the barometer.** At 1101 m the barometric formula gives
1013 × exp(−1101/8400) ≈ 886 hPa, and the logger measured 895–898 hPa on site — the
difference being ordinary weather. It also explains the excursions in §4.3: the readings at
926 hPa correspond to ≈840 m, which is León city, 261 m below the site.

All UTC, with the Sun's altitude as predicted:

Totality lasted **1 m 46.4 s** (limb-corrected), obscuration 100.00%, magnitude 1.01408,
83.89% of the umbral depth, 24.1 km from the centre line of a 299.1 km path.

**This is the best clock in the dataset, and it validates the FITS timestamps to the
second.** The first `SCI_ladder` frame is stamped `DATE-OBS 2026-08-12T18:28:21` — 14 seconds
after C2, i.e. just inside totality, which is exactly where a ladder would start. Two further
checks agree: `DATE-OBS` matches the same header's `JD_UTC` to 2–3 s (the exposure mid-point
offset), and every capture *folder* name matches `DATE-OBS` to the second, so **the
acquisition laptop was running on UTC**, not local time.

**The eclipse was at 9.7°, not 11°.** Header `OBJCTALT` reads 10.56° at that moment — 0.7–0.9°
above the prediction, which is far too much for refraction (0.09° at this altitude) and is
presumably mount pointing error. Trust the eclipse record. It puts the eclipse at essentially
the **same altitude as the Horizon-1 field (9.62°)**, which is what that field was for: it is
named `H1_eclipse_altaz`.

## 4. Environment, and the timezone that had to be established

`leon_temp_press_humid.csv` is UTF-16, tab separated, 4086 readings at 30 s, spanning
**17:45 UTC on 11 August to 03:48 UTC on 13 August**.

**Its times are local — CEST, UTC+2 — and its own header field is wrong about that.** The
header block says `Original time zone UTC+1`, which is a device setting with no knowledge of
daylight saving; peninsular Spain runs CET (UTC+1) in winter and **CEST (UTC+2) from the last
Sunday in March to the last Sunday in October**, so 11–13 August is UTC+2. An earlier draft of
this document took the header field at face value and was an hour out throughout. Three
independent checks settle it:

1. **The owner verified the logger's finish stamp against a laptop clock** at download.
2. **The diurnal peak.** Solar noon at this site is ≈13:22 UTC, and the temperature maximum
   should follow it by two to three hours. The logger's maximum (38.7 °C) is at 17:40 raw,
   which is 15:40 UTC at +2 — textbook. At +1 it would be 16:40 and at +0 17:40, both too
   late for a temperature peak.
3. **The eclipse cooling.** Read at +2, the record shows 33.5 °C at C1, **30.5 °C at
   totality** and 24.8 °C by C4 — a 3 °C drop into totality, in the right place.

### 4.1 Why two timescales: there were two laptops

Not an inconsistency, and worth recording so nobody "fixes" it later:

- **The acquisition laptop runs solely on UTC** — SharpCap and the CPWI mount software both
  on that machine, with no timezone choice offered. That is why `DATE-OBS`, `JD_UTC` and the
  capture folder names all agree, and why folder names are directly comparable with FITS
  timestamps.
- **The logger was started and stopped from a different laptop**, a general-purpose machine
  on the ordinary Windows default of following local time automatically. So its clock carried
  CEST.

The rule that falls out: **anything written by the acquisition chain is UTC; the environment
CSV is the only local-time source in the campaign.**

### 4.2 Where the logger physically was

The owner's placement log, which explains everything the record does:

| period | placement | what it measures |
|---|---|---|
| 11 Aug night (pre-eclipse calibrations) | cardboard box under a bush | see below |
| 12 Aug day, through the eclipse | same box, out of direct sunshine | least reliable |
| **12 Aug 19:15 UTC onwards** | **on the telescope's tripod spreader** | **true air temperature** |
| 12–13 Aug night (post-eclipse) | on the spreader | true air temperature |

The move is visible to the sample. At 19:14→19:16 UTC the **humidity steps 22.0 → 31.8% in a
single two-minute sample**, while the **temperature does not step at all** — it continues a
smooth evening decline through the move, dips to 20.1 °C at 19:54 as the sensor's thermal mass
equilibrates, and settles at 22.8 °C from 20:10.

That is a direct measurement of what the box was doing, and it is the useful result:

- **At night the box tracked air temperature.** If it had held a thermal offset, leaving it
  would have produced a temperature step, and there is none. So the pre-eclipse night
  temperatures (Zenith 1, DARKS, FLATS, Horizon 1) are usable as air temperature.
- **The box's humidity is not air humidity** — it read about ten percentage points low. Every
  boxed RH figure below is an underestimate. This matters far less than it sounds: at optical
  wavelengths water vapour is a small negative term in the refractivity, and ten points of RH
  at these temperatures is worth a few hundredths of a percent of the total refraction.
  (Humidity is a large term for *radio* refraction, not this.)
- **The daytime box readings are the weak ones**, because daytime is when a box does something:
  reduced ventilation, and shade is not the same as free air.

Comparing matched hours across the two nights gives 0.2–1.4 °C between them, but those are
different nights with different weather, so that figure bounds the box bias rather than
measuring it. The absence of a step at the move is the better evidence.

### 4.3 The excursions are a location signal too

Pressure barely notices being in a box, so it stays good throughout — and it changes when the
*altitude* does, which is how the record says when the kit left the site:

Bracketed figures are the ones the placement compromises: **every boxed humidity** (about ten
points low), and the **daytime box temperatures**, which is the one case where shade is not
free air. The night box temperatures are unbracketed because §4.2 measured that the box held
no thermal offset after dark. Pressure is good in every row.

**The refraction mosaic has the best environmental data of the whole campaign** — logger on
the spreader, in free air, throughout. That is a fortunate alignment: the one dataset captured
specifically to *verify a refraction model* is the one whose temperature, humidity and
pressure can all be trusted.

**The eclipse window is the weakest**, and it is the one that would matter most. Its pressure
(896.8 hPa) is good; its 30.5 °C is a shaded-box reading during the day and probably runs a
little warm against free air, and the 3 °C drop into totality is probably damped by the box.
The cost is bounded and small: see §5.

**How much does the eclipse temperature uncertainty cost?** Refraction scales as P/T with T
in kelvin, so being wrong by 3 K out of ~303 K is 1%. On the ~330″ of absolute refraction at
9.7° that is ~3″, which the plate solve absorbs; on the 0.5″ of *curvature* that survives the
fit (§5) it is 0.006″. **Immaterial to the term that matters** — worth saying so, to stop
anyone chasing it.

Use the windowed values, never the file mean: the whole-file pressure mean of 907.6 hPa is
10 hPa off the site, because it averages the site with the city.

**The pipeline's defaults are `observation_pressure = 1010.0`, `observation_temp = 10.0` and
`observation_height = 0.0`.** The site is 1101 m at 895–898 hPa. Refraction scales as P/T, so
the defaults overstate it by **15.7% on the observing nights and 17.2% at the eclipse**.

## 5. What that costs, and the part that is easy to overstate

Absolute refraction is mostly harmless: a uniform shift is absorbed by the plate solve, and a
uniform gradient across the field is absorbed by the fit's linear terms. What survives is the
**curvature** of refraction across the field — and near the horizon refraction is strongly
nonlinear in altitude, so over a 0.94° field it is not small.

The camera was set roughly horizon-parallel with the Sun low in the frame, so the gradient
runs along the short axis. At the windowed site conditions of §4.4, and with a plate scale of
2.216″/px from `FOCALLEN` 350 and `XPIXSZ` 3.76:

| altitude | dataset | total across field | **curvature the fit cannot absorb** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.00° | mosaic M01/M80, the lowest | 70.4″ | **2.28″ (1.03 px)** |
| 9.50° | eclipse C3 | 25.2″ | **0.53″ (0.24 px)** |
| 9.62° | Horizon 1 | 25.3″ | **0.52″ (0.24 px)** |
| **9.70°** | **eclipse maximum** | **24.4″** | **0.50″ (0.23 px)** |
| 9.90° | eclipse C2 | 23.5″ | **0.48″ (0.21 px)** |
| 11.60° | Horizon 2 (+2°) | 18.3″ | 0.32″ (0.15 px) |
| 20.00° | eclipse C1 | 6.7″ | 0.07″ (0.03 px) |
| 45.89° | mosaic M20 | 1.6″ | 0.01″ |
| 79.92° | Zenith | 0.9″ | 0.00″ |
| 88.92° | mosaic M40, the highest | 0.9″ | 0.00″ |

Against the 111–116 mas these fields actually fitted at (§2.6), the **0.50″ at eclipse maximum
is about 4× the achieved rms**, and a full pixel at the bottom of the mosaic. That confirms the
report from the field: at these altitudes vertical refraction is the dominant term.

**But the parameter error is the smaller half, and it would be easy to claim otherwise.**
Using the defaults — 1010 hPa, 10 °C, 0 m — instead of the measured site changes the
*curvature* by only:

| altitude | measured curvature | with the defaults | error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.00° | 2.28″ | 2.67″ | +0.39″ (0.18 px) |
| 9.70° | 0.50″ | 0.60″ | +0.10″ (0.05 px) |
| 11.60° | 0.32″ | 0.38″ | +0.06″ (0.03 px) |

So there are two separate findings, and only the first is large:

1. **Refraction must be modelled at all.** `enable_corrections_ref` is `False` by default, and
   the app window cannot enable it — it is classic-UI-only. Not modelling it leaves 0.5–2″ of
   curvature in the residuals at horizon altitudes, or absorbs it into the fitted distortion
   coefficients, which contaminates the very thing the zenith fields exist to measure.
2. **Once it is modelled, feed it the real site.** Worth 0.1–0.4″ at these altitudes — small
   beside the first item, but still 1–3× the achieved rms, and nearly free. Note that the
   site must come from `actual leon site.JPG` and the CSV, **not** from `OBSLAT`/`OBSLONG`,
   which are 17.8 km out, and there is no altitude keyword in the headers at all (§3).

> **What the timezone correction changed, honestly.** An earlier draft read the logger as
> UTC+1, which sampled each window an hour early and put the eclipse at 33.5 °C instead of
> 30.5 °C. Redone at UTC+2, the curvature at horizon altitudes moves from 0.52″ to 0.50″ and
> the parameter error stays at 0.10″. **The conclusions did not depend on the error** — a 3 K
> temperature shift is 1% of 300 K, and this whole section is about a term that is 4× the
> noise floor. The table is now right, but the finding was never at risk.

### 5.1 The zenith/horizon pairing is the right design

Falls out of the table above and is worth stating. At 80° the refraction curvature is 0.00 px,
so **the zenith fields measure distortion cleanly**. At 9.6-9.7° it is 0.23-0.24 px, so the
horizon fields measure refraction — provided the distortion is held fixed at what the zenith
fields found.

The mechanism for that already exists: `distortion_fixed_coefficients` and
`distortion_reference_files`. Both are classic-UI-only, and both sit in Phase 2 of
[`UI_ROADMAP.md`](UI_ROADMAP.md). This campaign is the argument for promoting them.

The user reports the telescope was stable in rotation and plate scale across the 24 hours,
which is what makes transferring a distortion solution between the two nights legitimate;
polar alignment drifted from ~1′ to ~3′, which does not affect a per-field fit. Camera
rotation was ~3–4° off horizontal rather than 0°; that is a roll term the fit already solves
for, so it costs nothing beyond a slightly oblique refraction axis.

## 8. The two zenith calibration nights (added 2026-08-20)

The zenith runs on 2026-08-11 and 2026-08-12 -- the night before and the night after the
eclipse -- are the distortion calibration the eclipse reduction rests on. Raw frames in
`I:\FRA500 Leon Aug 2026\Zenith`, output under `I:\MEE_output\v1.3.6`. Same instrument as
Portland (FRA500 + 0.7x, ASI2600MM Pro), same 55 mm reducer spacing, 4 s at gain 101, six
fields of 30 frames each night.

### 8.1 Leon against Portland: the mount is fixed, the optics are not

| | Portland 2026-07-29 | Leon 08-11 | Leon 08-12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| header-to-solved pointing | 1.10-1.55 deg | **0.07-0.16 deg** | -- |

The polar alignment shows exactly where it should. Drift falls by a factor of five and

**But the PSF still grows the same way with radius**, which is the point that matters:

| radius | Portland | Leon 08-11 | Leon 08-12 |

(*Portland's corner figure was measured on a single frame, the Leon ones on stacks; the

filter, and it survived the move.**

### 8.2 Why night 2 is worse -- five explanations tested and rejected

Night 2's rms is consistently higher: mean 171 mas against 126, a factor 1.36. The excess is
real and none of the obvious causes survives contact with the data.

| candidate | test | result |
|---|---|---|
| deeper detection, fainter stars | magnitude distributions | **rejected** -- medians 12.13 vs 12.19, bin fractions match; the gap is 1.31-1.62x in *every* magnitude bin |
| crowding (night 2 matched 69% more stars, nearer the galactic plane) | rms vs nearest-neighbour separation | **rejected** -- isolated stars (nn > 40 px) still show 163 vs 114 mas, ratio 1.43; rms is flat with separation within each night |
| cubic underfitting | refit both nights at quintic under v1.3.9 | **rejected** -- quintic gains only 2-8%, gap persists at 1.29-1.31x |
| dither / trailing / alignment | per-frame alignment residuals, all 12 field-nights | **rejected, and backwards** -- night 2 had *less* dither (8.6 vs 9.1 px mean) and a *lower* alignment residual (0.210 vs 0.260 px max) |
| sky brightness or noise | background and robust noise per frame | **rejected** -- 511-513 ADU and 7.41 ADU noise, identical |

What is different, and it is the only thing found: **`FOCUSPOS` 17049 on night 1 against 17041
on night 2** -- eight focuser steps -- with `FOCTEMP` 0.7 C warmer. Constant within each night,
so focus was set once and held. Independent corroboration that the optical configuration
genuinely differed: the fitted plate scale is **2.20780 against 2.20738 arcsec/px, a 190 ppm
shift, against a within-night field-to-field scatter of 5-6 ppm**. That is 32 sigma; the two
nights were not the same optical system.

**How much of the 1.40x that explains: about half, and no more.** The stacked PSF is 4% worse
on axis and 11% worse at the corner on night 2. In the background-limited regime centroid error
scales as FWHM squared, so 1.108 at the corner predicts about 1.23 in rms against 1.52
observed; on the median PSF it predicts 1.09-1.19 against 1.40. **The remainder is not
accounted for.** Recorded as open rather than guessed at.

One suggestive detail for whoever picks it up: the corner radial/tangential axis ratio is
**1.122 on night 1 and 1.026 on night 2**. Astigmatism reverses sign through focus and passes
through a round "circle of least confusion" between, so this is consistent with the two nights
sitting on opposite sides of the astigmatic balance -- eight steps apart, with the roundest
corners not being the sharpest ones. That would say the focus criterion to optimise is not
on-axis sharpness.

**Practical consequence.** A Bahtinov mask on a central star sets on-axis focus precisely,
which is exactly what it is for -- and on a system with residual field curvature that is the
wrong target. Night 2 was focused just as carefully as night 1 and came out worse everywhere.
Until the spacing is corrected, focus should be judged on a whole-frame metric (median FWHM
across the field, or the corner value) rather than on axis. **Eight focuser steps cost 36% of
the rms**, which is a larger lever than anything else measured on these two nights.

### 8.3 A separate finding: the alignment residual grows with the dither

The stage-1 archives record per-frame `shifts_px` and `rms_px`, and across **12 of 12
field-nights** the alignment residual rises with accumulated shift, median correlation
**+0.94**. Where the field walks 9-17 px it climbs from 0.07 to 0.20-0.35 px; where the walk
is 3 px it stays flat at 0.07-0.14 px and the correlation vanishes (0.04-0.14).
`corr(dither span, max alignment residual) = +0.73`.

That is the F2 argument measured directly on the calibration data: a pure translation cannot
register two frames of a distorted field that are offset from one another, so the residual
grows with the offset.

**With the caveat that it does not propagate here.** `corr(dither span, final field rms)` is
**+0.01** and `corr(max alignment residual, final field rms)` is **-0.29**. So on this data
per-frame affine alignment would improve the stack's internal consistency without measurably
improving the astrometry. F2 remains worth doing -- and this is cleaner evidence for it than
anything in section 1.5 -- but it should be sold as removing a known defect, not as recovering
rms on fields like these.

### 8.4 For the eclipse reduction

- **Both nights are usable, and 08-11 is the better calibration** -- 126 mas against 171, and
  closer in configuration to nothing in particular, since the eclipse day had its own focus
  session. If the eclipse frames' `FOCUSPOS` is recorded, match the calibration night to it.
  That is now a checkable thing rather than a guess.
- **Re-reduce under v1.3.9 rather than trusting the v1.3.6 numbers.** Re-running the same
  archives gives 97-116 mas on night 1 against 120-134 recorded, and 123-152 on night 2
  against 154-185 -- a 15-20% improvement from the pipeline alone, before any change to the
  data.
- **Use quintic.** It is worth 2-8% here and costs nothing.
- **Consider cropping.** Portland's static wall fell from 202 to 148 mas at r < 1400 px
  (section 13.6 there). The same test has not been run on Leon and should be, because it is the
  one lever that moved a term averaging cannot.
- **The 190 ppm plate-scale difference between nights is a warning about transferring
  coefficients.** Distortion held fixed from one night and applied to another is being applied
  to a measurably different optical system. Whether 190 ppm matters at the deflection level is
  worth computing before the eclipse reduction leans on a cross-night calibration.

## 9. What the zenith fields are actually for: high-order stability

The rms comparison in section 8 is the wrong figure of merit and the question that prompted it
is the right one. The plate scale and the other low-order terms come from the eclipse-day
calibration field, so what the night-time zenith fields have to deliver is the **nonlinear**
distortion, transferred to the eclipse frames. Compared as *evaluated displacement* rather than
raw coefficients, per section 1.1 of `ROADMAP.md`.

### 9.1 The nonlinear field is far more stable than the rms suggests

Azimuthally averaged displacement from **cubic terms only**, arcsec, six fields per run:

| run | r = 500 px | 1000 | 1400 | 2000 | 3000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Portland 07-29 | 0.013 +-0.9% | 0.107 +-0.9% | 0.294 +-0.9% | 0.856 +-0.9% | 2.889 +-0.9% |
| Leon 08-11 | 0.015 +-1.3% | 0.120 +-1.3% | 0.330 +-1.3% | 0.963 +-1.3% | 3.250 +-1.3% |
| Leon 08-12 | 0.014 +-1.9% | 0.114 +-1.9% | 0.313 +-1.9% | 0.913 +-1.9% | 3.082 +-1.9% |

Three things, and they are much better news than the rms figures:

- **Within-night repeatability is 0.9-1.9%**, across six fields on three separate runs. That
  sits inside Bruns' between-session figures of 0.9% and 2.6% (`ROADMAP.md` section 3a), on
  different equipment. The pipeline is measuring the cubic to about 1%.
- **Night to night, the ratio is 0.949 at every radius** -- 500 px to 3000 px, identical to
  three decimals. So the cubic **shape is unchanged** and only its **amplitude** moved, by
  **5.1%**. That is a much cleaner statement than "night 2 was worse".
- **Portland to Leon is +12.5%** (2.889 to 3.250 at r = 3000), so shipping the rig across the
  Atlantic and rebuilding the mount changed the cubic amplitude by an eighth.

### 9.2 The number that matters, against the requirement

Bruns' analysis needs the cubic coefficient to **1%** for milli-arcsecond work. So:

| | amplitude change | against the 1% requirement |
|---|---|---|
| field to field, same night | 0.9-1.9% | **at the limit** |
| night to night, same site | **5.1%** | **5x too large** |
| site to site | 12.5% | 12x too large |

**A cross-night calibration transfer injects about 5% into the cubic.** And the only
configuration difference found between those nights was eight focuser steps (section 8.2),
which gives a sensitivity of roughly **0.64% of cubic amplitude per focuser step**. To hold the
cubic to 1% the focus would have to be repeatable to about **1.5 steps** -- which a Bahtinov
mask will not deliver.

For comparison, the same eight steps moved the plate scale by 190 ppm, so **the cubic term is
about 270 times more focus-sensitive than the plate scale**. That is the reason the zenith
fields cannot be treated as a one-off calibration.

**What follows for the eclipse reduction:**

1. **Match the calibration to the eclipse focus.** If the eclipse frames carry `FOCUSPOS`,
   choose the zenith night whose value is closest, and record which was used. That is a
   checkable decision now rather than a guess.
2. **Better: calibrate at the eclipse focus.** A zenith set taken without touching the focuser
   between it and the eclipse frames removes the dominant transfer error entirely.
3. **Do not average the two nights** without first deciding whether a 5% amplitude spread is
   acceptable. Averaging two configurations gives the cubic of neither.
4. **Propagate it.** 5% of the cubic at the radii the eclipse stars actually occupy is a
   computable contribution to the Einstein coefficient, and it has not been computed. It
   belongs in `bench/ERROR_BUDGET.md`.

### 9.3 Focusing off-axis: right in principle, little gain on this train

The proposal: put the Bahtinov star about a third of the way along the long axis rather than at
the centre, so the residual curvature is split rather than dumped entirely at the edge.

**The geometry is exactly right.** With field curvature the best-focus surface goes as
`z(r) = z0 + c*r^2`, so focusing at radius `r_f` leaves defocus `c*(r_f^2 - r^2)`. Minimising
either the maximum or the area-weighted mean square of that over a usable radius `R` gives

    r_f = R / sqrt(2) = 0.707 * R

For a usable radius of 1400 px -- which is where Portland's static wall bottomed out
(`PORTLAND_2026-07-29.md` section 13.6) -- that is **990 px, or 32% of the half-long-axis**.
One third along the long axis is the correct answer to the question as posed.

**But on this optical train it buys almost nothing**, because the radial growth is not
defocus-dominated. Fitting the measured FWHM(r) three ways:

| night | model | fit residual |
|---|---|---|
| 08-11 | `A + B r^4` (defocus/astigmatism, blur ∝ r^2) | 0.182" |
| 08-11 | `A + C r^2` (blur ∝ r) | **0.067"** |
| 08-11 | both | **0.032"** |
| 08-12 | `A + B r^4` | 0.293" |
| 08-12 | `A + C r^2` | **0.037"** |
| 08-12 | both | 0.037" |

A pure defocus law fits badly. The data wants blur growing **linearly** with field radius,
which is coma or lateral colour -- not field curvature or astigmatism, both of which grow as
`r^2`. Corroborating that, the radial/tangential axis ratio rises with radius (1.02 to 1.15 on
night 1), which a symmetric defocus cannot produce.

Optimising the area-weighted FWHM over focus position on the fitted model:

| usable radius | on-axis | best r_f | gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1400 px | 4.19" | 990 px | **0.1%** |
| 2088 px | 4.45" | 1476 px | 0.6% |
| full frame | 5.56" | 2657 px | 4.1% |

So: **negligible inside the usable field, and a few percent only if the corners are kept**.

**Which is consistent with the filter still being the root cause, and strengthens the case for
fixing the spacing rather than working around it.** A reducer at the wrong back focus does not
only curve the field -- it introduces coma as well, and coma does not move with the focuser.
That is why refocusing, on-axis or off, has so little leverage here: the aberration that
dominates is the one focus cannot reach. Your point about manufacturing tolerance stands, but
it argues for measuring the best spacing rather than for compensating with focus.

**The decisive experiment, and it is cheap.** Take a focus sweep on a zenith field -- five to
seven exposures at spaced `FOCUSPOS` values, say +/-30 steps in steps of 10 -- and measure
FWHM(r) at each. Ten minutes at the telescope. That gives the movable fraction of the radial
growth directly, without any fitting assumption, and it simultaneously calibrates the
0.64%-per-step cubic sensitivity from section 9.2, which is the more valuable of the two
numbers. Do that before the next campaign and both questions are settled from data.

## 10. The eclipse was shot at a different focus, and what that means

### 10.1 Three corrections to section 9

**Lateral colour is out.** With a Sloan r' filter (about 140 nm wide) the chromatic term is
suppressed, so the blur growing linearly with field radius is **coma**, which is what a reducer
at the wrong back focus produces. The conclusion of 9.3 is unchanged -- coma does not move with
the focuser -- but the mechanism is now singular rather than "coma or lateral colour".

**There was no centre-versus-edge trade between the nights**, and section 8.2 implied one by
quoting a radial rms table without uncertainties. With bootstrap errors:

| radius | 08-11 | 08-12 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-700 px | 93.3 +-17.0 (252) | 76.3 +-10.2 (419) | **-0.9 sigma, not significant** |
| 700-1400 | 74.3 +-8.5 | 106.6 +-6.8 | 3.0 sigma |
| 1400-2100 | 109.9 +-7.7 | 130.7 +-4.9 | 2.3 sigma |
| 2100-2800 | 117.5 +-5.9 | 167.5 +-4.4 | 6.8 sigma |
| 2800-3800 | 156.6 +-5.3 | 238.7 +-4.8 | 11.5 sigma |

Night 2 is worse everywhere it is measurable, and the apparent better centre was 252 stars of
noise. That agrees with the stacked PSF (4.22" against 4.05" on axis), so the two measurements
were never in conflict -- the first table simply should have carried errors.

**The per-step cubic sensitivity in 9.2 is weaker than stated.** 5.1% over eight steps is only
about three times the 1.3-1.9% within-night scatter, so "0.64% per step" is a ratio of two
poorly determined numbers. What survives is the direction and the order of magnitude.

### 10.2 The focus positions, measured

`FOCUSPOS` is constant within every run, so each was focused once and held:

| run | FOCUSPOS | FOCTEMP | steps from eclipse |
|---|---|---|---|
| zenith 08-11 | 17049 | 29.85-29.99 C | **121** |
| zenith 08-12 | 17041 | 30.56-30.88 C | **129** |
| **eclipse + CAL_piLeo** | **17170** | **36.80-37.13 C** | -- |

So the eclipse was **not** shot near either calibration night: it sits 121-129 steps away, at a
temperature 6-7 C higher. Two things follow immediately.

**The observer refocused, and was right to.** 121 steps for 7.1 C is **17 steps/C**, and 129
for 6.3 C is 20.5 -- a consistent thermal coefficient. Tube length, lens spacings and glass
index all move with temperature, and at f/3.9 the depth of focus is small enough that 7 C
matters. Not refocusing would have cost far more than refocusing did.

**But the eight steps between the two nights are not thermal.** Night 2 was 0.8 C *warmer* and
its focuser sat 8 steps *lower* -- the opposite sign to the eclipse trend. So that difference is
**Bahtinov repeatability, about 8 steps**, not temperature tracking. Useful to know: it sets the
floor on how well any focus-dependent quantity can be reproduced between sessions.

### 10.3 Why this breaks the calibration transfer

The focuser and the reducer-to-sensor spacing are **the same degree of freedom**. Moving the
focuser 121 steps moves the flattener's working distance by 121 steps' worth, so the eclipse
frames carry a different field-aberration state from either zenith night -- not merely a
different focus.

The scale of it is measurable rather than assumed. Eight steps moved the fitted plate scale by
**190 ppm**, so the spacing changes about 24 ppm per step, and 121 steps is roughly **2900 ppm
of the 55 mm spacing** -- about 0.15 mm. Set against the 0.94 mm the unallowed filter already
costs (`PORTLAND_2026-07-29.md` 13.5), that is a 15% change in an error that was already the
dominant optical term.

And eight steps moved the cubic amplitude 5.1%. **121 steps is fifteen times that baseline.**
Extrapolating linearly would give a nonsense answer, so the honest statement is the qualitative
one: *the nonlinear distortion at the eclipse focus is very unlikely to match either zenith
night at the 1% level Bruns requires, and no measurement here bounds how far off it is.*

The low-order terms are fine -- a 2900 ppm plate-scale shift is exactly what the eclipse-day
calibration field is for, and that was always the plan. It is the **cubic** that has no
substitute.

### 10.4 The mitigation exists, and it has a prerequisite

`I:\FRA500 Leon Aug 2026\Eclipse\CAL_piLeo` was taken at **FOCUSPOS 17170** -- the eclipse
focus exactly -- 39 frames at 0.3 s, 1.0 s and 2.0 s, gain 0, `FOCTEMP` 36.96 C, minutes
before the science frames. So a calibration at the correct optical state does exist.

Two obstacles, one of which is on the roadmap already:

1. **It sits at altitude 10.57 degrees.** At that altitude the *curvature* of vertical
   refraction across the field is 0.5-2 arcsec (section 5), which is several times the cubic
   displacement being measured at small radii. Fitting distortion to this field without
   modelling refraction would put the refraction into the cubic term. **That makes F7 --
   header harvest, site, and refraction on by default -- a prerequisite for using the only
   correctly focused calibration of the campaign**, not merely an accuracy improvement. It is
   already first on the v1.4.0 list; this is a second, independent reason.
2. **Thirty-nine frames across three exposures**, and the 2 s frames clip pi Leo (section 1).
   The usable set is the eleven 1.0 s frames plus perhaps the 0.3 s ones, which is a thin
   basis for a 20-parameter quintic. A cubic may be all it will support -- which is fine, since
   the cubic is the term at issue.

### 10.5 What to do

- **Reduce CAL_piLeo with refraction enabled and compare its cubic against both zenith
  nights.** That single measurement replaces every extrapolation in 10.3 with a number, and it
  is the one piece of analysis that most directly bounds the campaign's distortion error. It
  needs F7.
- **Do not transfer the zenith cubic to the eclipse frames without that comparison.** If they
  agree, the zenith calibration is validated and its better statistics can be used. If they
  disagree, CAL_piLeo is the only valid calibration and its precision sets the limit.
- **Record `FOCUSPOS` in the run summary.** It is in the frame headers, the pipeline never reads
  it, and it turned out to be the single most diagnostic number in this dataset. An F7 item.
- **For future campaigns: take the zenith calibration at the eclipse-day focus.** Refocusing for
  temperature is correct and should continue; the fix is to re-take a calibration field after
  refocusing, not to leave the focus alone. On the night before, that means matching the
  daytime temperature rather than the night's.
- **And Bruns' corner placement is the structural mitigation.** A cubic *error* is field-centred
  while the deflection is Sun-centred, so with the Sun away from the field centre the two do
  not project onto each other -- which is his argument for the corner (`ROADMAP.md` 3a). Under a
  focus-dependent cubic that argument gets stronger, and it applies equally to the mid-edge
  geometry Leon was testing.

## 11. CAL_piLeo reduced with refraction, and what it can and cannot calibrate

Done on `v1.4.0-dev`, because it required fixing the refraction correction first.

### 11.1 The refraction correction was broken, not merely disabled

`enable_corrections` raised `AttributeError: 'StarTable' object has no attribute 'c'` on the
first star table it was handed. `StarData` exposed its SkyCoord as an attribute `.c`;
`StarTable`, which replaced it, builds one lazily through `skycoord()`. The migration kept
every `get_*` accessor name -- the class comment says so explicitly -- but `.c` was an
attribute rather than a method and was missed. The write-back was the same story: `ret.c`,
`ret.haspm` and `ret._update_vectors()` are all `StarData` shapes, and `StarTable` uses
`__slots__` and would reject them.

So the astrometric corrections have been **inoperative for as long as the new catalogue layer
has existed**, and nothing caught it because they are off by default and reachable only from
the classic UI. This is why F7 is more than plumbing: the thing it would switch on does not
currently run.

Fixed on `v1.4.0-dev`: read through `skycoord()`, write back through the `select(slice(None))`
copy idiom the table itself uses in `at_epoch`, and set the epoch to the observation so a later
propagation cannot move the positions twice. 869 tests still pass.

### 11.2 It works, and it matters at this altitude

CAL_piLeo, 11 frames at 1.0 s, blob removal off, site 42.740470 N / -5.613780 E / 1101 m,
30.5 C / 896.6 hPa / 20.8% RH read from the logger at 20:29 local (section 4), wavelength
0.62 micron for Sloan r':

| | plate scale | rms | cubic at r=3000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| refraction off | 2.21372 | 439 mas | 7.266" |
| refraction on | **2.20600** | 437 mas | 7.631" |
| zenith 08-11 | 2.20780 | 126 mas | 3.250" |
| zenith 08-12 | 2.20738 | 171 mas | 3.082" |

The correction displaces stars by **1.27 arcsec** on average at a mean altitude of 9.93 deg.
Three things follow:

- **The rms barely moves** (439 to 437 mas), because a smooth refraction gradient is largely
  absorbable by the plate solve and the distortion polynomial. Judging refraction by the rms
  would conclude it does not matter. That is the trap.
- **The plate scale moves 3500 ppm**, from 2.21372 to 2.20600 -- and *towards* the zenith
  nights, from 2700 ppm away to about 800. That is a strong independent check that the
  correction is doing something physically right.
- **The cubic changes by exactly 5.0% at every radius** -- a pure amplitude shift, which is
  what a radial refraction term looks like. So without the correction, refraction is absorbed
  into the cubic coefficient, exactly as feared in section 10.4.

### 11.3 But CAL_piLeo cannot measure the cubic

It is **detection-limited at about 23-38 stars**, and no setting fixes that:

- Raising the catalogue depth does nothing: max magnitude 13, 14, 15 give 32, 32, 31 stars. The
  limit is what is detectable in a daylight sky at 10.5 deg altitude, not the catalogue.
- The **2.0 s frames fail entirely** -- "no star centroids found". The brightest star clips and
  the background is higher, so they are worse than the 1.0 s set, not deeper. The 1.0 s frames
  are the correctly exposed ones (section 1).
- Lowering the detection threshold trades one way for another: `sigma_subtract` 3.0 / 2.0 / 1.0
  gives 104 / 283 / 58 centroids and 23 / 25 / 29 stars into the fit.

And the cubic is demonstrably unconstrained. Three reductions of **the same frames**, differing
only in detection threshold:

| run | stars | plate scale | cubic r=1400 | cubic r=3000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sigma_subtract 3 | 23 | 2.20553 | 0.534" | 5.259" |
| sigma_subtract 2 | 25 | 2.20547 | 0.522" | 5.137" |
| sigma_subtract 1 | 29 | 2.20531 | 0.326" | 3.206" |
| **spread** | | **43 ppm** | **21%** | **21%** |

Against a zenith within-night scatter of **1.3-1.9%** on the cubic and **5-6 ppm** on the plate
scale. A 21% swing from changing nothing but a detection threshold is not a measurement.

**So the division of labour is confirmed, and it is not a choice.** CAL_piLeo measures the
plate scale -- 43 ppm reproducibility, adequate -- which is the job it was given. It cannot
supply the cubic, and no amount of care with this field will change that: 38 stars will not
determine 20 parameters.

### 11.4 Where that leaves the campaign

The chain closes badly, and it is better to know:

1. The cubic must come from the zenith nights, because CAL_piLeo cannot provide it.
2. The zenith nights were shot **121 and 129 focuser steps** from the eclipse (section 10.2),
   at a plate scale differing by about 800-950 ppm once refraction is accounted for.
3. That transfer error therefore **cannot be eliminated** -- only bounded, and nothing measured
   so far bounds it.

Three things would help, in order of value:

- **A focus sweep** (section 9.3) turns the transfer error into a measured number instead of an
  unknown. It is the single most valuable ten minutes available, because it converts
  "unbounded" into "bounded".
- **Bruns' geometry argument is the structural mitigation** and gets stronger here: a cubic
  error is field-centred, the deflection is Sun-centred, so with the Sun off the field centre
  they do not project onto each other. Under an unbounded cubic error that stops being a
  refinement and becomes the thing protecting the result.
- **Take the calibration at the eclipse focus next time.** A zenith set after refocusing to the
  expected daytime temperature would remove the whole chain. Refocusing between night and day
  is right -- the data shows 17-20 steps/C, and not refocusing would cost more -- but it has to
  be followed by a calibration at the new focus.

## 12. CAL_piLeo done properly: the plate scale to 7 ppm

### 12.1 Correction to 11.3: the 2 s frames are fine

Section 11.3 reported that the 2.0 s frames "fail entirely -- no star centroids found". That
was a reduction error, not a property of the data. The `2.0s` directory holds **two** capture
blocks and the glob picked up both:

| block | frames | span | altitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| `18_29_27` | 7 | 18:29:28 - 18:29:42 | 10.54 deg |
| `18_29_57` | 8 | 18:29:55 - 18:30:12 | 10.45 deg |

The second runs past the end of totality, so the two blocks are not the same sky and stacking
them together is what produced the failure. On `18_29_27` alone the 2 s set solves without
difficulty: **51 centroids, 35 matched** at the default threshold, and **59 / 43** at
`sigma_subtract = 1.5`. It is a usable set, and at the lower threshold it yields slightly more
stars than the 1 s set, as expected from the longer exposure.

### 12.2 The plate scale, which is what this field is for

Four independent reductions -- two exposures, three detection thresholds, different frame
counts -- all refraction-corrected:

| reduction | stars | linear plate scale | quadratic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 s x11, sigma 3 | 18 | **2.20478** | 2.20488 |
| 2.0 s x7, sigma 3 | 14 | **2.20479** | 2.20506 |
| 2.0 s x7, sigma 2 | 16 | **2.20475** | 2.20502 |
| 2.0 s x7, sigma 1.5 | 21 | **2.20478** | 2.20506 |
| **spread** | | **18 ppm total, ~7 ppm scatter** | ~80 ppm |

**Seven parts per million**, across different exposures and different detection settings. That
is the same order as the zenith fields' 5-6 ppm field-to-field scatter, achieved on a daylight
field at 10.5 degrees altitude with twenty stars. The eclipse-day calibration does its job.

And it gives the number that matters for the transfer: the eclipse-day plate scale is
**2.20478 against 2.20780 on 08-11 -- 1368 ppm lower**. That is a measured, robust difference
between the eclipse-day and zenith-night optical configurations, and it is exactly why the
low-order terms have to come from the daytime field rather than from the night before.

### 12.3 The order dependence, which quantifies the division of labour

Reproducibility across those same four reductions degrades sharply with order:

| order | reproducibility | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| linear | **7 ppm** | excellent |
| quadratic | ~80 ppm | usable |
| cubic | 195 ppm between exposures; **21% in amplitude** across thresholds | not a measurement |

So section 11.3's finding should be read as **quantitative confirmation of the design, not a
discovery of a problem**. That the cubic cannot come from a totality field with twenty-odd
stars is well understood -- it is precisely why the campaign takes night-time zenith fields for
the high-order terms and a daytime field for the low-order ones. What is new here is the
number: the split is clean at linear, holding at quadratic, and gone by cubic.

### 12.4 Focuser backlash, and what the step counts can carry

The ZWO EAF's backlash is around **15 steps**, and this telescope moves roughly **5-9 steps per
degree C**. Two consequences for section 10.2:

- **The 8-step difference between the two zenith nights is inside backlash.** It cannot be
  read as a real difference in commanded focus, and the two nights may well have been at the
  same optical point. What is *not* inside backlash is the **190 ppm plate-scale difference at
  32 sigma** -- so something optical did change between them, but attributing it to those eight
  steps was over-reading. The 0.8 C temperature difference predicts 4-7 steps, which is
  consistent with the observed 8 and with backlash noise; a thermal cause fits at least as well
  as a focus-position one.
- **The eclipse offset is larger than temperature alone explains.** 121-129 steps against a
  7 C difference predicts 35-63 steps at 5-9 steps/C, so the measured offset is two to three
  times that. Either the coefficient is higher on this train than the general figure -- the
  measurement here gives 17-20 steps/C -- or part of the move was not thermal. Worth resolving,
  because it changes how far the eclipse configuration really sat from the calibration.

### 12.5 A design note for the next calibration field

pi Leo spans a wide magnitude range, and that costs more than it gives. The brightest star
saturates at 2 s while most of the field needs the longer exposure, so no single exposure
serves the whole field and the useful star count is squeezed from both ends. For a field whose
purpose is the **linear and quadratic** terms, what matters is **breadth of coverage across the
sensor and the number of unsaturated stars** -- one very high signal-to-noise star adds little,
because the fit is limited by how well the field is sampled, not by the best star in it.

A field chosen for a narrow magnitude spread and even sensor coverage would let one exposure do
the whole job and would likely beat 7 ppm.

### 12.6 An interface gap found on the way

`--order` offers only `linear`, `cubic`, `quintic`, `septic`, while the code implements
`quadratic`, `quartic` and `sextic` as well (`mapping` in `distortion_polynomial.py`). Fitting
at quadratic -- the order this field is actually meant to supply -- required
`--set distortionOrder=quadratic` to bypass the choice list. That belongs on the F12 list: an
option the code supports that no interface offers.

## 13. Combining exposures, and the calibration field to choose next time

### 13.1 The 1 s and 2 s sets combine, and that is the best reduction

They had been reduced separately. Stacking them together is legitimate for astrometry -- a
centroid is exposure-independent for an unsaturated star, so mixing exposures changes the
weighting but not the positions -- and it is the best of everything tried:

| reduction | linear: stars | scale | quadratic: stars | scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 s x11 | 18 | 2.20478 | 19 | 2.20488 |
| 2.0 s x7, sigma 1.5 | 21 | 2.20478 | 24 | 2.20506 |
| **1 s + 2 s combined** | **25** | **2.20473** | **31** | **2.20479** |

Most stars at both orders, 50 matched in the plate solve against 41-43 for either set alone.
And it fixes something the separate fits did not: on its own each set's **quadratic** scale
drifts 40-130 ppm away from its linear one, because 19-24 stars cannot hold both. Combined,
linear and quadratic agree to **27 ppm** -- the extra stars stop the quadratic pulling the
scale around.

Across all five reductions the linear plate scale spans 2.20473 to 2.20479, **27 ppm total**,
about 9 ppm scatter. The figure to carry forward is **2.2047-2.2048 arcsec/px**, against
2.20780 on 08-11.

`--no-exposure-check` is required, since the frames genuinely differ in exposure; that flag
exists for the mislabelled-header case but serves here too.

### 13.2 The field was the wrong choice, and the numbers say why

The proposal -- **one exposure of 2 s or 4 s for the whole calibration window, on a field
offset to avoid the single bright star** -- is right, and the frames say how much headroom
there is:

| exposure | sky background | frame max | saturated pixels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 s | 1176 ADU (1.8% of full scale) | 18 352 | 0 |
| 1.0 s | 2839 ADU (4.3%) | 37 382 | 0 |
| 2.0 s | 5058 ADU (7.7%) | **65 535** | **1** |
| 4 s (extrapolated) | ~9500 ADU (14.5%) | -- | -- |

**The sky is nowhere near the limit.** At 4 s it would sit at about 14.5% of full scale, with
ample room above it. What forces the exposure ladder is a *single pixel* -- the brightest star
clipping at 2 s. One star is costing the entire calibration its exposure depth.

Nor would 4 s trail: differential refraction allows roughly 15 s at 10 degrees altitude
(section 5), and the mount was polar aligned to about 1 arcminute.

So a field chosen for a **narrow magnitude spread and even sensor coverage**, offset enough to
leave the bright star out, could use a single 4 s exposure for the whole window. That buys
about 0.75 magnitudes of depth over the 1 s frames -- a meaningful increase in star count at
these depths -- while removing the need to split the window across three exposures and then
recombine them afterwards.

The underlying principle, worth stating because it runs against instinct: for a field whose
purpose is the **linear and quadratic** terms, the fit is limited by **how well the field is
sampled**, not by the best star in it. A very high signal-to-noise star adds almost nothing and
costs the exposure depth that would have delivered a dozen more usable ones. Breadth of
coverage and count of unsaturated stars are what matter.

**For the next campaign:** choose the calibration field on those criteria, pick the exposure
from the sky background rather than the brightest star, and take the whole window at it. On
this evidence that should better the 7-9 ppm achieved here, and it would remove the
exposure-ladder bookkeeping entirely.

## 14. Two settings regimes, from the 2024 Station 1 reduction

The classic-UI settings used for the 2024 Station 1 analysis (MEE 2024 Results and
Conclusions; NP101is + ASI6200MM at f/5.0, against the FRA500 + ASI2600MM at f/3.9 here) show
something the repository has never written down: **a zenith field and a totality field are run
with materially different settings**, and the differences are not small.

| | zenith field | eclipse field | config key |
|---|---|---|---|
| sensitive stacking mode | **off** | **on** | `sensitive_mode_stack` |
| sensitive mode on stack | on | on | `centroid_gaussian_subtract` |
| sigma_thresh | 5.0 | **4.0** | `centroid_gaussian_thresh` |
| min_area (px) | 4 | **2** | `min_area` |
| **sigma_subtract** | 3.0 | **0.0** | `sigma_subtract` |
| remove centroids near edges | off | **on** | `remove_edgy_centroids` |
| blob removal | off | on, gap 2000 | `delete_saturated_blob` |
| **distortion fit tolerance** | **0.1"** | **20"** | `distortion_fit_tol` |
| max star magnitude | 15 | **12** | `max_star_mag_dist` |
| polynomial order | quintic | quintic | `distortionOrder` |
| fix order higher than | None | **constant** | `distortion_fixed_coefficients` |

The zenith UI itself carries the guidance in its label -- sensitive stacking mode is "use if
close to sun or moon; do not use for zenith or fields with >> 100 stars" -- but the rest is
undocumented practice.

Three of these matter enough to call out, because they are the difference between a totality
field reducing and not:

- **`sigma_subtract = 0.0`.** The default is 3.0, which subtracts three sigma from every pixel
  before detection. On a star-rich zenith field that is a sensible noise gate; on a field with
  twenty faint stars against a bright sky it removes much of what there is to find.
- **`distortion_fit_tol = 20` arcsec, against 0.1 for the zenith.** This is the matching
  tolerance, and 0.1" assumes a solve already good to a tenth of an arcsecond. A totality field
  with few stars and a poor initial solve cannot meet that, so a tight tolerance rejects almost
  everything before the fit begins. Two hundred times looser is not a fudge -- it is the
  acknowledgement that the first pass is rough.
- **`distortion_fixed_coefficients = constant`** on the eclipse field: every distortion
  coefficient is held from the reference files and only the constant is fitted. That is the
  calibration transfer made explicit, and it is the mechanism sections 9 to 12 are about.

**All of these live in section 3a's F12 category** -- reachable only from the classic UI, absent
from the app window, and several absent from the CLI's named flags. `sigma_subtract`,
`min_area`, `centroid_gaussian_thresh` and `distortion_fit_tol` have to be set through
`--set KEY=VALUE`, and nothing in the interface tells a user that a totality field needs
different values from a zenith one. For a campaign whose whole method is "calibrate on one kind
of field, measure on another", that is the wrong thing to leave undocumented.

**Recommendation for F12:** ship the two regimes as named presets -- "zenith / calibration
field" and "totality field" -- rather than expecting each observer to rediscover them. The
values above are a working starting point, from a reduction that produced a published result.

### 14.1 Applying the 2024 eclipse-field settings to CAL_piLeo

Tried on the combined 1 s + 2 s stack, with the blob removal left off since pi Leo is away
from the Sun. Stage 1 with `sensitive_mode_stack`, `centroid_gaussian_subtract`,
`centroid_gaussian_thresh 4.0`, `min_area 2`, `sigma_subtract 0.0`, `remove_edgy_centroids`:

| | centroids | matched in the solve |
|---|---|---|
| my best settings (`sigma_subtract 1.5`) | 56 | 50 |
| **2024 eclipse-field settings** | **119** | **94** |

`sigma_subtract = 0.0` is the lever. I had scanned 3.0, 2.0, 1.5 and 1.0 and never reached
zero, and it roughly doubles what the field yields. It is also much slower -- vastly more
candidates survive to be tested -- which is presumably why the default is what it is.

Stage 2, scanning the matching tolerance:

| `distortion_fit_tol` | order | stars | plate scale | rms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 95 | 2.20472 | 966 mas |
| 2 | quadratic | 96 | 2.20473 | 870 mas |
| 5 | linear | **103** | **2.20471** | 1153 mas |
| 5 | quadratic | **103** | **2.20472** | 1104 mas |
| 10, 20 | both | 103 | identical to tol 5 | identical |

Three things:

- **The tolerance saturates at 5 arcsec.** 5, 10 and 20 give byte-identical results, so the
  2024 value of 20 is generous rather than necessary -- it was chosen for a totality field
  whose initial solve is rough, and CAL_piLeo's is not. Anything above about 5 costs nothing
  and gains nothing here.
- **Star count rises from 25-31 to 103**, and the rms rises with it, to about 1 arcsec. That is
  not bad matching -- it saturates, so the extra stars are real -- it is the sensitive settings
  reaching faint, low-signal stars whose individual centroids are poor. For a **plate scale**
  that is a good trade: more stars, each noisier, and the mean is better determined. For a
  cubic it would not help.
- **The plate scale does not move.** Linear 2.20471 and quadratic 2.20472 now agree to
  **0.5 ppm**, against 27 ppm with 25-31 stars.

**Across every reduction of this field** -- 1 s, 2 s and combined; thresholds from 0.0 to 3.0;
tolerances from 1 to 20; 14 to 103 stars; linear and quadratic -- the plate scale spans
**2.20471 to 2.20479, 36 ppm**. It was already well determined at 25 stars; the 2024 settings
confirm it with four times as many and tighten the internal consistency by fifty-fold.

**So the value to carry into the eclipse reduction is 2.2047 arcsec/px**, against 2.20780 on
08-11 -- the eclipse-day configuration sitting about **1400 ppm** from the night before. That
is now measured five independent ways and is not going to move.

### 14.2 Three tolerance regimes, and why 20 arcsec was right in 2024

The 2017 Bruns zenith reduction adds a third setting to the pair in 14: **`distortion_fit_tol
= 0.2`**, cubic order, magnitude 13, rough-fit threshold 36, refraction on (10 C, 770 mbar,
0.4 humidity, 2400 m, 0.625 micron). So the tolerance is not one value with an exception -- it
is chosen per field, and the reasoning is what should be recorded:

| field | tol | why |
|---|---|---|
| 2017 zenith (Bruns) | **0.2"** | a clean zenith field fits to about 0.1", so 0.2 admits the good stars and rejects mismatches -- the setting for **accurate, low-noise cubic coefficients** |
| 2024 Station 1 eclipse | **20"** | the field **contains the Sun**, so the stars carrying the deflection are displaced from catalogue position *by the very signal being measured*. A tight tolerance would erase exactly the stars the experiment exists to measure. Compounded by a 287 x 191 arcmin field needing leeway at the outer edges |
| CAL_piLeo | see below | away from the Sun, no deflection, smaller field -- so neither 2024 reason applies |

That second row is the important one and I had it half right in 14.1: I read 20 arcsec as
"generous rather than necessary". It was necessary, for a reason that has nothing to do with
solve quality. **And the reason returns for the actual 2026 Leon eclipse field**, which also
contains the Sun -- so the loose tolerance is not a CAL_piLeo lesson to carry forward, it is
specifically an eclipse-field one.

**Measured on CAL_piLeo**, scanning the tolerance on the 119-centroid stack:

| tol | order | stars | plate scale | rms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | quadratic | **6** | 2.20503 | 0 mas (degenerate) |
| **0.5** | linear | 21 | 2.20470 | **309 mas** |
| **0.5** | quadratic | 27 | 2.20468 | **302 mas** |
| 1.0 | quadratic | 68 | 2.20476 | 612 mas |
| 2 | quadratic | 96 | 2.20473 | 870 mas |
| 5 | quadratic | 103 | 2.20472 | 1104 mas |

**0.2 arcsec is too tight for this field** -- six stars, and a quadratic through six stars
gives a meaningless zero residual. That is not a contradiction of the 2017 value: a clean
zenith field achieves about 0.1" per star, so 0.2" sits just above its noise, whereas
CAL_piLeo achieves about 300 mas at 10.5 degrees altitude in daylight, so 0.2" sits *below*
its noise and rejects legitimate stars.

**The rule that generalises is not a number.** The tolerance should sit a little above the
field's achievable per-star scatter: 0.2" for a zenith field fitting at 0.1", about 0.5" for
this one fitting at 0.3", and 20" for a field where the signal itself displaces the stars.
Setting it from the data rather than from habit is the F12 preset argument again, and it is
why "distortion fit tolerance" cannot sensibly have one default.

For CAL_piLeo the best compromise is **tol 0.5**: 21-27 stars at 300 mas, against 103 stars at
1100 mas. And the plate scale is indifferent to the whole argument -- 2.20468 to 2.20476 across
every tolerance from 0.5 to 20, which is the same 36 ppm band as everything else in section 13.
**2.2047 arcsec/px stands.**

## 15. The L/R transfer step, read from the 2017 output

`distortion_resultsL.txt` and `distortion_resultsR.txt` from the 2017 reanalysis show the
calibration transfer working, and the files prove the mechanism rather than describing it.

**The cubic coefficients are identical to every digit** in both files:

    x^3  0.19911423325936842      y^3  0.2757365908467087
    x^2*y 0.02826692142196996     x^2*y -0.05475748122315109
    x*y^2 -0.05201115646095876    x*y^2 -0.013767685705603373

while the **quadratic and linear terms differ** -- L has `x^2 = +0.0151`, R has `-0.0142`; L
has `x = 0.0695`, R `0.0902`. That is `fixed distortion order: quadratic` doing exactly what
the name says: everything **above** quadratic is held from the reference files, and constant,
linear and quadratic are solved per field.

And the reference is not one night's field but **fifteen** of them -- the
`fixed distortion reference files` entry lists fifteen `aug 19 calibration` results, averaged.

The result: **106 and 120 mas rms on 75 and 77 stars**, with L's nearest-neighbour error
correlation at **-0.0026** -- essentially zero spatially correlated residual, which is the
strongest single piece of evidence that the imported cubic is right for that field.

### 15.1 This changes the transfer exposure, and mostly for the better

Sections 9 to 12 treated the whole nonlinear field as transferring from the zenith night. It
does not: **only cubic and above transfer**, and quadratic is re-fitted on the eclipse day
along with the linear terms. Two consequences for Leon:

**Averaging solves the precision half.** Fifteen fields at roughly 1-2% each give about 0.4%
on the mean, which is how the 1% requirement in section 9.2 is actually met. Leon has six
zenith fields per night at 1.3-1.9% scatter, so the mean lands at **0.5-0.8%** -- inside the
requirement, and better than any single field suggests. The concern in 9.2 was overstated for
the *random* part.

**It does not solve the systematic half.** The 5.1% night-to-night amplitude shift and
whatever the 121-129 focuser steps to eclipse day contribute are offsets, not scatter, and
averaging six fields taken at one focus does nothing to them. That remains the open term.

**Re-fitting the quadratic absorbs some of it, and where it matters most.** On the Leon zenith
fields the cubic is about 99.5% of the nonlinear displacement at r = 3000 px, so re-fitting the
quadratic recovers almost nothing at the field edge. But at r = 500 px the quadratic carries
about 12% of it -- and small radii are where the deflection is largest. So the transfer is most
exposed exactly where the signal is weakest, and best protected where the signal is strongest.
That is a more comfortable position than section 12.4 implied, though it is a partial
mitigation rather than a fix.

### 15.2 What this means for the Leon reduction

The 2017 recipe is directly applicable, and now specified rather than remembered:

1. Reduce the six zenith fields of the chosen night, cubic order, `distortion_fit_tol 0.2`.
2. Pass all six as `distortion_reference_files`, with `distortion_fixed_coefficients =
   quadratic`, so the averaged cubic is imported and the lower orders are re-fitted.
3. Reduce the eclipse field with those settings plus a tolerance loose enough not to reject
   the deflected stars (section 14.2), refraction on with the measured site and weather.

The one judgement left is **which night**, and section 10 gives the criterion: match the
zenith `FOCUSPOS` to the eclipse's 17170. Both nights are 121-129 steps away, so neither is
close, and that gap is what the focus sweep would bound.

## 16. The full three-step method, from the 2017 settings

With the final step's settings the whole chain is now specified. It has never been written
down in this repository, and two of its values look wrong until the reason is stated.

| | step 1: zenith | step 2: L/R calibration | step 3: eclipse field |
|---|---|---|---|
| input | night-time zenith fields | L and R fields | the eclipse frames |
| `distortion_reference_files` | -- | the N zenith results, averaged | **L and R results** |
| `distortion_fixed_coefficients` | None | **quadratic** | **constant** |
| what is fitted | everything | constant, linear, quadratic | **the constant only** |
| `distortion_fit_tol` | **0.2"** | 0.2" | **999"** |
| `max_star_mag_dist` | 13 | 13 | **11** |
| `distortionOrder` | cubic | cubic | cubic |
| gravitational correction | off | off | **off, deliberately** |
| refraction | on | on | on |

So the distortion is built up once and then progressively frozen: the zenith fields determine
the cubic, the L/R fields import it and add the lower orders at the eclipse-day configuration,
and the eclipse field imports the entire model and solves only its own pointing offset.

**`distortion_fit_tol = 999` is the point, not a shortcut.** At the final step no star may be
rejected for sitting away from its catalogue position, because *that displacement is the
measurement*. A tolerance of any ordinary size would discard the stars nearest the Sun --
exactly the ones carrying the largest deflection -- and would do it silently, biasing the
result towards zero. 999 arcsec means "reject nothing". Section 14.2 worked out that the
tolerance tracks the field's achievable scatter; this is the case where that rule is suspended
entirely because the residual is the signal.

**Gravitational correction is off for the same reason, inverted.** `enable_gravitational_def`
exists and would remove the deflection from the catalogue positions. Leaving it off is
deliberate: the deflection stays in the residuals, and Tab 3 then fits the 1/R dependence of
what remains. Every other correction in the chain is switched on to remove a systematic; this
one is switched off so that the thing being measured survives to be measured.

**And `max_star_mag_dist` tightens to 11** at the final step, from 13 in calibration. Fainter
stars are helpful when fitting twenty polynomial coefficients and harmful when measuring one
number, because near the corona their centroids are the least reliable and each one enters the
1/R fit with equal weight.

This is also why the geometry argument holds. The distortion is a **field-centred polynomial**,
now entirely fixed; the deflection is **Sun-centred and 1/R**. Freezing everything but the
offset leaves precisely one functional form free to absorb the signal, and it is not the same
form as the distortion -- which is what makes the separation clean, and what Bruns' corner
placement is protecting (`ROADMAP.md` 3a).

The Tab 3 output plotting catalogue against measured positions is the visible end of this:
zoom in and the measured stars sit radially outward from the Sun, by an amount falling with
distance. That is the raw signal, before any fitting.

### 16.1 What this specifies for Leon

Steps 1 and 2 are settled by sections 12 to 15. Step 3 needs, for the Leon eclipse frames:

- `distortion_reference_files` = the CAL_piLeo result (which plays the L/R role: eclipse-day
  focus, low orders re-fitted)
- `distortion_fixed_coefficients = constant`
- `distortion_fit_tol = 999`
- `max_star_mag_dist` around 11
- gravitational correction **off**; refraction **on**, with the site and weather from section 4
- blob removal **on** for the eclipse field, sized from the corona (F14 would measure it)

The one structural difference from 2017: Bruns had **two** calibration fields either side of
the Sun (L and R), averaged. Leon has CAL_piLeo alone. Two fields bracketing the Sun constrain
the low-order terms across the region the eclipse frames occupy, where one field to one side
must extrapolate. Worth noting as a design point for 2027 -- and worth checking, when the
Leon eclipse reduction runs, whether the single-field extrapolation shows up as a gradient in
the 1/R residuals.

### 17.0 Why the grep missed them

**The labels and the keys do not share vocabulary, and one differs by dialect.** The checkbox
reads *Center* on Moon; the key is `object_centre_moon`. `grep -ci center mee2024/config.py`
returns **0**. The other label, *Cutoff radius (solar radii)*, maps to `limit_radial_sun_radii`
-- no shared word at all.

So searching `DEFAULT_OPTIONS` for the words on screen found nothing, and a missed grep got
written up as an absent feature. The lesson is narrow and worth keeping: **a grep that returns
nothing is evidence about the search string, not about the code.** Confirming a control is
absent means reading the layout function that builds the tab -- `UI_handler.py` had both
widgets in plain sight the whole time.

### 17.0.1 The real gap, which is much smaller

Three of the four are **classic-UI-only**. Neither `mee2024/ui/` nor `cli.py` mentions
`object_centre_moon`, `limit_radial_sun_radii` or `remove_double_stars_eclipse`; only
`eclipse_limiting_mag` reaches the CLI. So an eclipse reduction driven from the app window or
the command line cannot set a Sun-centred cutoff or centre on the Moon, and silently runs with
the defaults (`False`, 9.0). That is a genuine interface gap and it is the CLAUDE.md rule about
three front ends reaching one pipeline -- but it is not a lost feature, and the fix is exposure,
not restoration.

### 17.1 Limiting magnitude belongs in Tab 3, not Tab 2

Unchanged by the correction above. Moving it was right, and it resolves a tension in section
16. There, `max_star_mag_dist` was 13 for calibration and 11 for the eclipse step -- but those
are answers to two different questions being asked through one control:

- **Fitting distortion** wants every star it can get: twenty coefficients need constraining,
  and a faint star with a mediocre centroid still adds information about the field's shape.
- **Measuring the deflection** wants only good stars: it is one number, every star enters the
  1/R fit with comparable weight, and near the corona a faint star's centroid is the least
  reliable thing in the frame.

With the limit in Tab 3 both can be satisfied -- fit at 13, analyse at 11 or 12 -- rather than
compromising the fit to protect the measurement. **Magnitude is the S/N handle**, and it should
sit at the stage whose error budget it controls.

### 17.2 Two different crops, and they are not substitutes

Both exist. They solve different problems and neither substitutes for the other:

| | centred on | removes | in the code |
|---|---|---|---|
| `crop_circle` / `crop_circle_thresh` | **the field** | the soft sensor edge -- distortion the polynomial cannot model, and the degraded PSF | `distortion_fitter.py:127` |
| `limit_radial_sun_radii` | **the Sun** | stars where the deflection is too small to measure against the distortion error | `eclipse_analysis.py:144-146` |

Both matter for Leon and for different reasons. The field-centred crop is what took Portland's
static wall from 202 to 148 mas (`PORTLAND_2026-07-29.md` 13.6) and would address the soft edge
this rig has from the back-focus error. The Sun-centred cutoff is the operational form of
Bruns' argument -- he discards stars where the cubic distortion exceeds the deflection, and
since the deflection falls as 1/R while the distortion error grows with field radius, that
criterion is essentially a radius from the Sun. His paper puts the useful zone at **5 to 10
solar radii**, and the 9.0 shown sits squarely inside it.

**Recommendation:** expose both beyond the classic UI, and use both for Leon. They are the two
ways of saying "these stars are not worth using", and a campaign that places the Sun off-centre
-- corner or mid-edge -- needs both, because the field edge and the far-from-Sun region are then
no longer the same set of stars.
