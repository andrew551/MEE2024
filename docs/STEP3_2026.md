## S0 — the SCI_ladder, measured (2026-08-29)

### The ladder itself

**87 frames spanning 39.2 s (18:28:12.9 → 18:28:52.1 UTC), a symmetric exposure ladder
0.1 → 0.3 → 0.6 → 1.2 → 0.6 → 0.3 → 0.1 s**, gain 0 (the night sets use 101), plus one
~50 ms default-setting frame in `discard/` (corner sky 525 ADU against the 0.1 s blocks'
826 — about 0.064 s equivalent). Integration per tier: 4.6 / 6.9 / 7.2 / 6.0 s — comparable
by design, at four saturation radii. The science sequence occupies the **first third of
totality**; CAL_piLeo (18:29:17.5 → past C3) the last third; the **25.35 s** between them is
the slew. (An earlier draft said "18:29:19–53 … ~27 s", taking the CAL start from the
`1.0s/18_29_19` folder name; the earliest CAL frame is in `0.3s/18_29_17` and opens at
18:29:17.54. See "The totality time budget" at the end of this file for the full accounting.)

**The EXPTIME trap is confirmed on this dataset, five times.** Every frame whose header
disagrees with its folder has a corner-sky level matching its **folder**, not its header
(e.g. `0.1s/18:28:21.008`, header 0.30 s: sky 805 ADU against the 0.1 s block's 826 and the
0.3 s block's 1398). The folder organisation is the truth; the first frames after a SET
EXPOSURE change carry the new header on the old exposure.

**Saturation is entirely coronal and the 2 R⊙ inner cut survives every tier**: the clipped
region (99th-percentile radius of a mid-block frame) runs **1.38 / 1.57 / 1.71 / 1.86 R⊙**
for 0.1 / 0.3 / 0.6 / 1.2 s. No *star* comes near clipping — the brightest matched star
peaks at ~8 000 ADU in the 1.2 s frames against a 65 535 ceiling, so F16 has nothing to
reject on the science field at these exposures (it remains mandatory for CAL_piLeo and any
deeper future tier). The sky *falls* ~8 % through the science sequence (826 → 758 ADU at
0.1 s) — the approach to mid-totality; the 726 → 5700 ADU **rise** in the record belongs to
CAL_piLeo's half, approaching C3.

### The catalogue, projected onto the field

An empirical sky→pixel affine from the matched stars (5.2 px mean residual) puts the Sun at
RA 142.146° Dec +14.895° — 0.72° from frame centre, matching the recorded 0.74° — and
counts **150 catalogue stars G ≤ 11 on the sensor, 139 in 2–11 R⊙** (the record's 132, the
difference being edge effects). By annulus: 18 in 2–3 R⊙, 18 in 3–4, 38 in 4–6, 49 in 6–8,
16 in 8–11. The Sun-centre pixel used throughout S0 is the clipped-region centroid
(3110, 3204); the record's (3208, 3293) differs by 132 px = 0.31 R⊙ and the discrepancy is
an **open item** — it shifts every radius and therefore h.

### What detection reaches, and what limits it — three campaigns of stage 1

All runs: daytime centroid regime (`centroid_gaussian_subtract=True`, thresh 4.0,
`min_area=2`), per-tier stacks (the folder is the exposure truth), stage 2 importing the
zenith cubic at `distortion_fixed_coefficients=quadratic`, corrections ON, per-tier
exposure-weighted mid-times, `distortion_fit_tol 2.0` (the M3-validated diagnostic value —
the final `tol 999` waits on F16-per-frame and robust per-star aggregation, per the plan).

**Raw frames, wide blob margins** (hull + 650 px — inherited, see the trap below): three
tiers solve, 47-star union, V ≤ 10.48, **nothing inside 2.64 R⊙**, 2 of the 36 catalogue
stars in 2–4 R⊙, **h = 33.0 R⊙²** against the design's 19.8. δL/L scales with h: a 1.67×
sensitivity penalty concentrated exactly where the 1/r signal is strongest.

**Raw frames, default margins** (hull + 130 px): the deep tiers improve, but the 0.1 s tier
**fails to platesolve** — its smaller saturated hull exposes the steep unsaturated rim, 107
of 160 detections land in the 1.5–2.0 R⊙ ring, and the solver feeds on them
brightest-first. **The blob margin is a knob with no good setting**: wide walls off the
inner annulus, tight feeds the solver junk, and the shallowest tier — the one that reaches
deepest — is hurt worst.

**Bruns-style coronal subtraction** (`tools/step3_s0_blursub.py`, prototyped from the 2017
paper's ECLIPSE pre-step): per tier, the unshifted mean of the raw frames is blurred with a
10 px Gaussian — hiding the stars, keeping the coronal shape — and subtracted from every
frame (2000 ADU pedestal; raw-saturated pixels re-imposed at 65 535 so the blob machinery
still masks the core). Three iterations, each of which taught something:

1. Tight margins (hull + 40 px) everywhere: deep tiers solve for the first time at such
   margins; shallow tiers get *worse* than raw (1190 detections at 0.1 s, 1146 inside
   2 R⊙).
2. Registering the model to each frame's corona by phase correlation (the mount drifted
   2.04 px between the 0.1 s tier's two blocks): **changed nothing, to the digit.** The
   residual is not a displacement error.
3. The subtracted stack's own statistics located the real obstacle: the field is flat to
   **4–8 ADU beyond 2.2 R⊙** — the subtraction works — but a band at 1.2–1.8 R⊙ carries
   110–264 ADU of **coronal fine structure**: streamer rays and the clip-boundary kink, at
   star scale, the same filaments visible in Bruns' fig 8(c)/(d). No smooth background
   model removes structure at star scale. **~2 R⊙ is a physical inner limit for this
   corona, not a parameter choice — and it coincides with the design's inner cut.** With
   per-tier masks sized to put the exclusion at ~2.0 R⊙ (margins 200/150/90/40 px), **all
   four tiers solve**.

### Traps recorded (each cost this session real time)

- **The CLI loads the interactive `MEE_config.txt` underneath its `--set` overrides.** The
  first S0 run silently inherited `blob_radius_extra=500 / centroid_gap_blob=150` from a
  previous classic-UI session — a 650 px exclusion that walled off 2–4 R⊙ and was initially
  misread as a detection-physics problem. Pin *every* stage-1 parameter in a driver.
- **Killing a driver script does not kill its running pipeline child.** An orphaned stage-1
  subprocess survived a kill, wrote a blob-off archive into a freshly-cleaned tree, and the
  resumable driver adopted it — a 1.2 s "failure" that was really a poisoned input. Check
  for orphans before relaunching into the same output tree.
- `delete_saturated_blob=True` is mandatory on this field (the CAL_piLeo recipe has it off
  — that field has no Sun in it). With it off, the coronal rim supplies hundreds of
  star-like detections and the solver dies.

### S0 addendum — V4: the forbidden region, solve inheritance, and an honest floor
(2026-08-29, after Douglas' challenges; supersedes three claims above)

**The "~2 R⊙ physical inner limit" above is WITHDRAWN as stated.** Douglas challenged it
twice and both challenges measured out. The 1.2–1.5 R⊙ σ = 264 ADU was inflated by the
blob's own darkening step; the honest MAD on un-blobbed subtracted frames is **75 ADU at
1.5–1.7 R⊙, 32 at 1.7–1.9, 17 at 1.9–2.1**. And the MEE reduction of Bruns 2017 matched
V 7.78 at 1.49 R⊙ and V 7.15 at 1.63 — two bright stars inside 2 R⊙, then nothing until
V 10.57 at 2.08. The floor is **brightness-dependent**, not a wall, and 2017 was never
better in kind: it had bright stars close in, and V3's mask never let Leon try.

**The Sun centre is (3171, 3232)** — the astropy ephemeris through the fitted cubic. The
clipped-region centroid used above (3110, 3204) is 67 px off (streamers bias it); the
plan's (3208, 3293) is 71 px off the other way. All S0 radii above carry that ~0.15 R⊙
uncertainty; V4 onward uses the ephemeris value.

**V4** (`tools/step3_s0_v4.py`, outputs `step3_s0_v4/`) drops the convex-hull blob
entirely — Douglas' design: a Sun-centred **forbidden disk** painted flat at the pedestal,
radius max(1.25 R⊙, the tier's measured saturation radius + 20 px) = 1.47/1.63/1.76/1.91
R⊙ per tier, residual saturated streamer tips dilated 10 px and painted too. Detection is
otherwise free. Results: the deep tiers solve (0.6 s: 257 centroids → 38 matched; 1.2 s:
101 → 26, rms 0.517 ″ — the best of any S0 run); the shallow tiers' *plate solves* die
(673 and 474 centroids — fake stars near the disk edge dominate the brightest-N list the
solver feeds on). Scored offline through the deep tier's solution (a cubic sky→px fit from
45 pipeline-matched anchors, 0.90 px residual — a LINEAR affine leaves 5.3 px on this
field, the refraction compression, and silently drops matches), the fakes cost nothing:
**detection is filtered by catalogue matching, the second and more rigorous filter**
(Douglas' framing). The fake-star problem is a *solver* problem only, and the solver fix
is inheritance — the shallow tiers taking the deep tier's solution, which the pipeline
cannot yet do (logged with F21/F22 as the eclipse case).

### Why the below-Sun anchor is not yet in any fit — mechanism found (2026-08-29)

Three suspects were eliminated by measurement: not the edge trim (the code keeps points
3–16 px from the edge, and the star is in every detection list — an earlier claim here was
wrong), not the double filter (`is_double(10″) = False`), not the fit tolerance (absent at
tol 3, 5, and 999 alike). The measured fact: **no star below py = 3825 ever matches**, at
any tolerance. The cause is in `match_centroids`: candidate association projects
detections through the **rough linear solve**, whose error on this refraction-compressed
field grows toward the frame edge and crosses the 36 ″ `rough_match_threshhold` at about
that line. Raising the threshold to 80 ″ is not a fix — the nearest-neighbour association
then mismatches wholesale (pre-outlier rms 21.4 ″, 43 of 44 candidates rejected, fit
collapsed). The proper fix is the matcher's own TODO: associate with the **fitted** model
(two-pass: fit on safely-matched stars, re-match the remainder through the result), which
is the S2 tool and the F21/F22-family pipeline item. Until then the anchor enters by
manual join — its pixel position is measured in every tier and its catalogue identity is
unambiguous.

Bonus finding from the same hunt: **"V 9.0 @ 1.87" is a flagged double** (companion
within 10 ″) — the pipeline excludes it from matching by design, closing that question.
Three different exclusion mechanisms in one field: a fake, a double, and an
edge-of-rough-model casualty.

**Dark library verdict (Douglas' doubt resolved): correct and usable.** All six gain-0
masters: median 502.4 ADU against recorded bias 500.9 (dark current negligible, as it
must be at gain 0, +10 °C, ≤ 2 s), MAD 0.56 ADU, hot-pixel counts 5 972 → 6 592 growing
monotonically with exposure, CCD-TEMP 9.6–12.4 °C against the science setpoint 10 °C,
same camera ID, 49–50 frames each, `mee2024-callib` format v1 — and `calibration.py` is
unchanged since the v1.3.7 line, so the format is current. The hot-pixel vetting layer
builds on these as they stand.

### The two-pass rematch works, the anchor is in — and it measures the edge field
(2026-08-29, late; `tools/step3_rematch.py`)

The tool reconstructs the pipeline's own pixel→sky chain from a finished stage-2 archive
(`apply_corrections` → `linear_transform` → `to_polar`, coefficients and q from the zip)
and re-matches **every** detection against the AstroCorrect-corrected catalogue at the
observation epoch. Two things made it honest: a **round-trip gate** — the reconstruction
must reproduce the already-matched stars' stored RA/DEC(obs), and it does, to
**0.0000 ″** once the convention scan found the chain (polynomial in the original frame,
axis flip only at the rotation step) — and per-pass hygiene learned the hard way in three
iterations: catalogue **proper motions must be propagated to epoch** (unpropagated
positions are 1–2 ″ off — the reason this tool's own first pass missed the anchor; the
*pipeline* propagates pm correctly, and its failure remains the rough-linear boundary,
compounded by the anchor's ~2.4 ″ offset exceeding tol 2.0 — triply doomed in all), **doubles filtered**
(the first pass smuggled the known V 8.97 double past stage 3's only filter), and
**blends rejected** (two catalogue stars claiming one detection at px 1224, 64).

The sensitivity pair, on vetted (outlier-dropped) star sets:

| tier | vetted only | + pass-2 stars, no anchor | **+ anchor** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.6 s | 2.08 ″ | 2.33 ″ (41 stars) | **2.65 ″** |
| 1.2 s | 1.71 ″ | 1.87 ″ (25 stars) | **2.43 ″** |

**The anchor pulls L up by +0.33 / +0.56 ″.** Read correctly, this is a measurement, not
a disappointment: the anchor's total offset is 2.0–2.4 ″ against an expected deflection
of 0.81 ″ at 2.17 R⊙, so ~1.2–1.6 ″ of its displacement is *not* deflection — and it
sits at the frame's bottom edge, which is the **lowest-altitude sightline in the field**,
where the uncancelled refraction differential and any model-transfer edge error both
peak. The same pattern shows in the other pass-2 recoveries (all near frame edges,
separations 1.4–2.4 ″, all pulling L upward): **the field's edges are displaced outward
relative to the model — a smooth unmodelled field, which is precisely what S1's nuisance
estimator exists to absorb.** The anchor's leverage becomes usable when the nuisance
field can separate a smooth vertical/edge term from 1/r; until then its inclusion is a
sensitivity number, not a better L. The preliminary range stands at **1.7–2.7 ″
depending on star admission policy** — which is itself the cleanest statement yet of why
the estimator, not more stars, is the critical path.

## S1 — the nuisance estimator, built and gated (2026-08-29, late)

`tools/step3_s1_estimator.py`: one linear model over 2N residual components — [N1, N2,
Θ, L·(1/R)û] plus a smooth **vertical** nuisance field (sensor ŷ, which the CAL affine
puts 3.1° from the local vertical; M3 measured the wavefield vertically polarised at
V/H ≈ 2.3), amplitude a low-order 2D polynomial. Geometry and the base fit inherit from
`m5_projection.py`, whose basis recovers an injected L = 1.000 exactly.

**A methodological trap caught by its own too-good result.** The first gate projected the
night residuals through a degree-3 smoothing surface before the estimator saw them — and
a full *vector* degree-3 nuisance then produced nulls of exactly 0.0000″, because it
spans the surface's function space *by construction*. Circular; withdrawn. Gate v2 fits
the **un-smoothed per-star medians on the night field's own stars**, wavefield
patchiness included, with the bootstrap over per-star errors as the floor:

### Einstein vs Newton, and the 2027 exposure design (2026-08-29, assessment)

**Where L = 1.98 ± 0.60 (stat) ± 0.33 (atm) ″ stands against the physics.** Total σ ≈
0.68 ″. Newton's corpuscular half-deflection is 0.875 ″; Einstein's 1.751 ″. Leon 2026
therefore sits **0.3 σ from Einstein and 1.6 σ from Newton** — consistent with GR,
disfavouring Newton, but *not yet* the Eddington discrimination (≥3 σ needs total
σ ≤ 0.29 ″). Improvements still available in this data: fainter stars via mag-12
matching with care (stat toward ~0.5 ″), a GLS error model with the measured wavefield
covariance, a gated patch-scale nuisance (the ±0.33 might shave to ~0.25). Realistic
2026 floor: total σ ≈ 0.45–0.55 ″ → ~2 σ from Newton. **The wall is the one-sided
calibration under an airmass-5.9 wavefield — measured, not assumed.** Leon at 9.6° was
the hard-mode experiment; its yield is the method and the atmosphere's characterisation.

**2027 (2027-08-02, totality 5 m 38 s at alt +56°) changes every limiting term at once**:
airmass 1.2 (vs 5.9) puts the site above M4's measured 10° validity boundary with
~50 ppm-class model residuals and near-zenith wavefield (~0.06 ″ quasi-static vs 0.28 ″);
338 s of totality (vs ~100 usable) allows the L/R calibration *during* totality,
interleaved L–SCI–R–SCI — Douglas' joint contemporaneous nuisance design realised; and
the patch decorrelation problem shrinks with the wavefield itself. Bruns reached ±3 % of
L at alt ~54° in 2017; **sub-0.1 ″ is a credible 2027 target → Einstein/Newton at ≥8 σ.**

**Exposure recommendation for 2027** (from the 2026 measurements): keep **at least three
science tiers — not for photons but for the cross-tier consistency vet**, which needs ≥3
witnesses per star (the vet that auto-killed V 9.10 ran on exactly that redundancy; the
0.3 s tier's real 2026 contribution was third-witness duty plus anchor detection, its
photons being redundant). A geometric ladder **0.5 / 1.5 / 4.5 s** (Douglas' proposal) is
right, **plus a deep ~9 s block** that 338 s easily affords — with the caveat that at
airmass 1.2 the sharper PSF and lower extinction make **bright-star clipping, not coronal
sky, the binding constraint on the long tiers: F16-per-frame becomes mandatory equipment,
not hygiene**. Repeat the ladder several times through totality: the quasi-static
wavefield evolves over minutes, so temporal spread converts it from a floor into an
averageable term. Calibration fields: chosen **without** bright stars (the 2026 L field's
single bright star is what forced its short exposures — a field-selection cost, avoidable
by moving half a degree), single deeper exposure (~2 s × N), both sides, interleaved.

Of the six kept CAL blocks (39 frames, 44.90 s), **60 % of the integration is inside

### The slew: 9.796°, and the mount was half as fast as the script assumed

The mount's own reported pointing, from the FITS headers (JNOW), first and last frames of each
field:

| | RA | Dec |
|---|---|---|
| `SCI_ladder` | 143.0260° | +15.2746° |
| `CAL_piLeo` | 149.8214° | +8.0815° |
| **axis motion** | **+6.7961° (+407.8′)** | **−7.1925° (−431.5′)** |

**On-sky great circle: 9.7958°.** Being an EQ mount both axes moved, and **Dec was the longer
axis at 7.19°** — so Dec, not the on-sky separation, sets the slew time if the axes ran
together. East–west on sky was 6.6554° (`ΔRA × cos(mean Dec)`); altitude barely changed
(+0.106°, the two fields being altitude-matched by design), azimuth by −9.889°.

This is what the script asked for. Its `OFFSET 34.3 29.3` → `OFFSET 442 -402` (RA-coordinate
arcmin) implies 6.7950° / −7.1883° / 9.7902° on-sky — **the mount landed within 0.006° of
the commanded move**. The script's own header quotes "9.73 deg", which is 0.06° low because it
converted with `cos(Dec_sun) = 0.9659` rather than `cos(mean Dec of the two fields) = 0.9789`.
Immaterial for pointing; corrected here for the record.

**True slew time.** Taking the script's stated settling at face value — SharpCap's 5 s hardware
settle inside `MOUNT GOTO`, plus its own `DELAY 5` — the 25.35 s decomposes as:

| | |
|---|---|
| last science frame read out and saved | ~0.30 s |
| **mount motion (slew + goto approach)** | **~14.3 s** |
| SharpCap hardware settling | 5.00 s |
| `DELAY 5` | 5.00 s |
| `SET EXPOSURE` + capture start | ~0.75 s |

The 0.75 s is not a guess: the same operation is measured six times inside the science ladder,
where the gap between one block's last shutter close and the next block's start runs
0.43–0.93 s (median ~0.75; the first transition is an outlier at 1.89 s). So **the mount took
about 14.3 s**, and the honest range is 13.9–14.8 s depending on how the overheads are
charged.

**That is an effective 0.50 °/s on the limiting Dec axis, or 0.69 °/s on the great circle.**
The script budgeted "slew motion 5-8 s" from an assumed AVX rate of ~4 °/s; the mount ran
**6–8× slower than that assumption** and about twice as slow as the budget's slow end. The
slew segment therefore overran its 15–18 s allowance by 7–10 s — **7–9 % of totality**, and
the single largest recoverable loss in the run. The record contains no prior measurement to
contradict this: the Portland Moon run measured *settling* (§6 there: sufficient, with room to
spare) and inter-field cadence for small offsets, never a large-angle slew rate. **For 2027,
9.8° at 0.5 °/s is the number to budget from, not a catalogue slew rate** — and it should be
measured on the actual rig at low altitude before the day, which is what the script's own
"MEASURE AVX slew + decay on the Moon run" line asked for and did not get.

One caveat, and it is not resolvable from the frames: if SharpCap's hardware settling was not
in fact 5 s on the eclipse laptop — the script flags it as "VERIFY", and the day-of checklist
claims it was confirmed — then the split between settling and motion moves one-for-one. The
25.35 s total is measured; the 14.3 s is measured *given* 10 s of settling.

### The 10 s of settling was not generous — it was marginal (2026-09-01)

The script's "Moon run measures true vibration decay at low alt" never happened, but the
calibration frames measure it retrospectively: track star centroids through each capture
block and fit a drift rate. A mount still settling decays block to block; steady tracking
error and differential refraction do not. `tools/step3_settling.py`.

| block | t after mount reported slew done | drift | scatter | stars |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cycle 1, 1.0 s | 18–24 s | **0.901 ″/s** | 0.151″ | 4 |
| cycle 1, 2.0 s | 21–35 s | **0.256 ″/s** | 0.106″ | 4 |
| cycle 2, 2.0 s | 48–58 s | 0.132 ″/s | 0.141″ | 1 |
| cycle 3 onward | 77 s+ | — | — | too few |

**The AVX was still moving when the calibration sequence started.** The drift decays 0.90 →
0.26 → 0.13 ″/s; a constant term cannot do that. The asymptote is where it should be: ~0.09 ″/s
of differential refraction (3″ per 0.1° of altitude, and the field was setting at 0.0031 °/s)
on top of tracking error — 0.13–0.26 ″/s corresponds to ~0.5–1° of polar misalignment, which is
what a daytime solar sync gives. So the *decaying* part, ~0.5 ″/s still present at t + 15 s, is
settle, and the mount's "slew complete" report was optimistic by tens of seconds.

What that cost: a 1.0 s frame at t + 18–24 s trails 0.9″ = 0.41 px, about 20 % of the 2.1 px
FWHM. **It does not bias the plate scale** — a uniform trail is a translation, identical for
every star, so centroids stay unbiased — but it broadens the PSF and inflates centroid noise,
and is a plausible contributor to the CAL field's 0.5323″ rms. The trail is on the six 1.0 s
frames of `18_29_19`, which are 6 of the canonical 16.

Confidence is limited and should stay that way: **1–4 usable stars per block**, and the decay
cannot be followed past ~60 s because the post-C3 sky washes the field out. The direction of the
result is solid; the time constant (order 10–25 s) is not.

**Two method traps, both of which produced a confidently wrong answer first**, recorded because
they will recur:

1. **Phase correlation is unusable on these frames.** The ASI2600's hot pixels are a *fixed
   sensor pattern*, far more correlated frame to frame than a sparse star field, so the
   correlation locks onto them and reports zero motion regardless of the sky. It passed an
   injected-shift test to 0.001 px and still returned **0.00 px of drift over 215 s**, against
   Portland's measured ±4–7″ of periodic error. That impossible zero is what exposed it.
2. **A source must span ≥ 4 connected pixels to be a star.** Stars here are ~2.1 px FWHM, hot
   pixels are single. Without that cut the "stars" are hot pixels and the answer is zero again.

The general lesson for any future settling or drift measurement on this rig: **validate against
a quantity that cannot be zero.** An injected-shift test only proves the estimator can move; it
does not prove it is looking at the sky.

### What an AM5 would have done, and what settling it needs

The Leon mount is the **Celestron AVX** ([`PORTLAND_2026-07-29.md`](PORTLAND_2026-07-29.md)
§ rig). Its headline rate is 4 °/s; it delivered **0.68 °/s aggregate** over this GOTO. That gap
is not anomalous in kind — headline rates never describe a GOTO, which also pays acceleration
ramps, the **two-stage final approach** a worm-gear mount needs to take up backlash from a
consistent direction, and the driver's refusal to report "complete" until that approach ends. A
factor of 2–4 between headline and aggregate is ordinary; **6× is at the slow end of normal for
an AVX**, and the settling measurement above says even the "complete" report was early.

A **ZWO AM5** is a strain-wave (harmonic) drive. The decisive difference is not its higher
headline rate (6 °/s) but that **it has no backlash, so it needs no backlash-takeup approach** —
which is where most of the AVX's 14.3 s went. For this move (9.796° on sky, 7.19° on the
limiting Dec axis) that is ~1.2 s at rate, **~2–3 s realistic end to end** with ramps and the
driver handshake. Payload is a non-issue: 13 kg without counterweight against a ~5 kg rig.

**Settling, conservatively.** Set the criterion first: smear ≤ 0.1 FWHM. At this field's
4.7–5.3″ FWHM and 2.2054 ″/px that is **≤ 0.5″ over the longest exposure**, so the residual rate
must be ≤ 0.25 ″/s for a 2 s frame and **≤ 0.10 ″/s for the 4.8 s deep tier 2027 wants**. A
strain-wave head has no gear-lash relaxation, so its settle is tripod-and-OTA ringing — a few Hz,
damping in 1–3 s on a stiff tripod, longer on a light one, and longer again if the AM5 is run
counterweight-free with the centre of gravity off-axis. **Budget 5 s**, which is exactly
SharpCap's default hardware settling — so the change to the script is to keep that and **delete
the `DELAY 5`**, not to add anything.

That gives **~8 s for the field change against Leon's 25.35 s**. Spending the recovered 17.4 s
at the calibration sequence's 74 % duty adds ~12.9 s of integration: **64.6 s open of 106.8 s,
a 60 % duty cycle against the measured 48 %** — or, put where it lands, calibration integration
inside totality rising from 26.90 s to ~39.8 s, **+48 %**.

Two caveats, and they matter more than the arithmetic. **The AM5 does not win on tracking.**
Harmonic drives carry larger, faster periodic error than a good worm — order ±10–20″ over a
period of minutes, so peak rates of 0.2–0.5 ″/s, which is the same order as what the AVX
actually delivered here. **On an eclipse you cannot guide**, so that is an unmitigated term for
either mount, and it sits right at the 0.25 ″/s smear budget for a 2 s frame. And **5 s is a
planning figure, not a measurement**: settling is a property of the tripod and OTA, not the
mount head, so it has to be measured on the actual rig — shoot a continuous short-exposure
sequence straight after a representative slew and fit the drift decay, which is what
`tools/step3_settling.py` does and what the 2026 script asked for and never got.

### The trigger: what the first frame actually shows

### The ladder syntax: what it cost, why, and the correction

`SET EXPOSURE TO x` immediately followed by `CAPTURE n LIVE FRAMES`, in `LIVE MODE`, is the
single defect behind every acquisition anomaly in this dataset. The live stream free-runs; the
exposure change is asynchronous with respect to both the stream and the capture counter. Three
distinct symptoms, one cause:

1. **Headers lie at block boundaries.** SharpCap writes `EXPTIME` from the camera's setting *at
   save time*, not at exposure time. Leading frames of a new block carry the new value on old
   pixels (`0.3s/18_28_21` files 00001–00002, really 0.1 s; `0.1s/18_28_45` files 00001–00002,
   really 0.3 s; `1.0s/18_29_19` file 00001, really 0.3 s; `2.0s/18_29_27` file 00001, really
   1.0 s) — and the *trailing* form exists too: `0.6s/18_28_26` file 00006 is a genuine 0.6 s
   frame stamped 1.2 s. Five blocks, seven frames. `DATE-AVG` and `DATE-END` are derived from
   `EXPTIME`, so they are wrong on exactly those frames.
2. **Frames are silently never written.** The block's file numbering runs to the commanded `n`
   but files are absent: `0.1s/18_28_13` stops at 00023 of 24, `1.2s/18_28_29` has no 00001 of
   6, `0.3s/18_28_41` stops at 00011 of 12, and on the calibration side `1.0s/18_29_51` and
   `discard/18_31_19` each stop at 00005 of 6. **Five frames commanded and never written**, of
   which the expensive one is the missing 1.2 s frame — 1.20 s, 71 % of the science ladder's
   entire 1.70 s shortfall.
3. **The rogue frame.** The very first `CAPTURE` grabbed whatever was already in the live
   buffer — the manual crescent-viewing exposure, ~0.064 s. `SCI_ladder/discard/00001.fits`.

There is a fourth cost that has nothing to do with correctness: **the six exposure changes cost
about 5.3 s of dead time** (0.43–0.93 s each, plus 1.89 s at the first), which is 13 % of the
39 s ladder and more than the 1.2 s frame that was lost.

**The truth was always recoverable, and from three independent sources**: corner sky level (S0),
frame-to-frame cadence (above), and — not previously used — **SharpCap's own
`*.CameraSettings.txt` sidecar in every capture folder**, which records `Exposure=` as commanded
and a `TimeStamp=` for the block start. `1.2s/18_28_29` reads `Exposure=1.200s`,
`TimeStamp=18:28:29.03`; `0.3s/18_28_21` reads `Exposure=300.0000ms`, `TimeStamp=18:28:21.19`
while its file 00001 is stamped 18:28:21.008 — **the first saved frame predates its own block's
start**. That sidecar is the documentary basis for "the folder is the truth", and it pins
intended-vs-captured per block without any photometry. Any future harvest should read it.

**The correction was diagnosed on 2026-08-17 and never written down here.** Douglas, in
`I:\MEE_transcripts\2026-08-17_exe-v136-release-review_577bb01f.md`, on first inspecting the
folders: the script *"needs a 2 sec delay after Set Exposure in order to allow the frame buffer
to clear"*, because *"there is a lag between acquisition and writing"* and the strays *"were put
into the following folder and had to be manually moved backward"*. That is the mechanism, more
precisely stated than the "asynchronous exposure change" framing used above, and **the frames
now confirm it independently**: a trap file's `DATE-OBS` *precedes the receiving block's own
`CameraSettings.txt` TimeStamp* (`0.3s/18_28_21` file 00001 at 18:28:21.008 against a block
start of 18:28:21.19). A frame written into a folder that did not exist when it was acquired is
a write-lag, not a mis-set exposure. The same message recorded the scope call that still
stands: automating the cleanup "is not general enough to warrant the effort".

**Does `DELAY 2` make sense? Mechanism and placement yes, cost depends on the eclipse.**

- **Placement is right.** After `SET EXPOSURE`, i.e. before `CAPTURE`, is where it works: the
  writer flushes the previous block's backlog before the new folder is created, and the new
  exposure takes effect before frames are counted. It fixes symptoms 2 and 3 and the *leading*
  form of symptom 1 — everything that cost data. It will **not** fix the trailing form
  (`0.6s/18_28_26` #00006, a true 0.6 s frame stamped 1.2 s), because `SET EXPOSURE` has
  already fired by then; that would need a second delay on the other side, and it is not worth
  one — right folder, right pixels, wrong header only.
- **2 s is generous but the right kind of generous.** The write-lag itself is ~0.285 s per
  52 MB frame at the measured 183 MB/s, so a one-to-two frame backlog clears in 0.3–0.6 s and
  2 s is 3–7× margin. The evidence argues against tuning it finer: **gap length does not
  predict the failure.** The transition that produced the worst trap (0.1 s → 0.3 s) had the
  *longest* natural gap of the ladder at 1.70 s, while transitions with 0.57–0.92 s gaps came
  through clean. A failure that is not gap-predictable wants a robust number, not a fitted one.
- **The cost is the whole argument.** Seven `SET EXPOSURE` commands × 2 s = 14 s, against
  ~5.3 s of transition overhead already being paid — so **+8.7 s net, up to +14 s if fully
  additive**, to recover the 1.70 s of integration the five lost frames represent. On Leon's
  106.8 s that is 8–13 % of totality bought for 1.6 % back, and **in pure integration terms it
  is a bad trade**. It is not a bad trade in *result* terms: integration was never the binding
  constraint here (the calibration field's §5; the 2027 assessment's "depth buys stars, not
  per-star precision"), while the disorder cost a superseded canonical plate scale — the
  17-frame stack contained a transition artefact and had to be redone as 16, moving the answer
  −11.7 ppm — plus a permanent trap for every future analyst.
- **On 2027's 338 s of totality the same 14 s is 4 %, and the argument stops being close.**
  **Adopt `DELAY 2` after every `SET EXPOSURE` for 2027.** For a short totality, 1 s probably
  suffices and halves the cost, but nothing here proves it; if certainty matters more than
  8 s, pay the 2 s.

**Two things this supersedes, both proposed in an earlier draft of this section and both
wrong:**

1. **Do not go monotonic to cut the number of transitions.** Measured: the symmetric ordering
   holds all four tier exposure-weighted mid-times within **2.43 s** of each other across a
   39.0 s ladder (0.1 s at +18.99 s, 1.2 s at +19.63 s, 0.6 s at +19.92 s, 0.3 s at +21.43 s).
   The same frames laid out as four contiguous blocks would spread those mid-times over
   **26.45 s — 11× worse**. The tiers must sample the same atmosphere for the cross-tier
   consistency vet to mean anything, and that vet is what auto-killed G 9.10. **The symmetry is
   the point of the design; the six boundaries are its price.** Keep it and pay them.
2. **Over-capturing `n+2` does not substitute for the delay.** Under the write-lag mechanism
   the strays land in the *following* folder, so over-capture restores the frame *count* but
   leaves the misfiling exactly as it was — the hand-sorting still has to happen. It is a
   complement to the delay, not an alternative to it.

**And regardless of the delay: never trust `EXPTIME`, `DATE-AVG` or `DATE-END` at a block
boundary.** A delay makes the trap rarer, not impossible. The folder-is-truth rule and the
sidecar cross-check belong in the harvest code, not in each analyst's memory.

None of this changes a measured number in the Leon reduction: the folder organisation already
encodes the corrected truth, and S0/S2 were computed on it.

### LOOP 8 TIMES: two were needed, three would have been right

The calibration cycle measures **29.7 s** steady-state (cycle-to-cycle starts: 27.4, 29.8, 29.4,
29.6, 29.8, 29.9, 29.8 s — the first is short because it opens directly). Calibration began at
C2 + 70.44 s, leaving **36.36 s** to C3. Cycle 1 filled 27.05 s of it; cycle 2 supplied the
remaining 9 frames before the C3 cut. **Two cycles covered totality.**

Two would also have sufficed under the *planned* budget (calibration starting at C2 + 55–60 s,
leaving 44–49 s), and even against an instant slew — calibration starting at C2 + 45 s, leaving
62 s — **three cycles cover every case**. So `LOOP 3 TIMES` was the right setting, and the
uncertainty in the slew time is fully absorbed by the third.

Five of the eight cycles were therefore surplus to totality. **They were not a mistake**: the
script says so in advance — "8 cycles ~ 3.5 min: remaining totality, C3, and the
brightening-sky tail (deliberate: star-visibility-vs-partial-phase data)" — and the data was
duly used, [`CAL_PILEO_STEP2.md`](CAL_PILEO_STEP2.md) measuring the post-C3 frames at ≤ 5 ppm
and giving the exclusion criterion its photometric statement. The cost was 129 frames
(~6.7 GB) and 2.5 minutes before the refit-the-filter prompt, with the scope parked east of an
unfiltered Sun. For 2027, where totality is 338 s and the calibration is interleaved rather
than terminal, the loop count stops being a tail and becomes part of the science budget.

### Caveats on the absolute numbers

`DATE-OBS` is the acquisition PC's system clock, not GPS-disciplined. **Relative timings
within the run are good to milliseconds; the absolute ones inherit whatever that laptop's
clock offset was, which the campaign has never bounded better than "seconds".** So 24.80 s and
26.90 s of integration are solid, and 5.83 s and 25.35 s are solid as intervals; what is soft
is their placement against C2 and C3. A second-level clock error would move the SCI and CAL
windows together, not relative to each other.

The tabulated contacts give totality as 106.8 s; the same record's summary line quotes
1 m 46.4 s limb-corrected. The 0.4 s difference is left standing — the budget has to close
against the contacts, and it does, to 0.00 s.

### What this says about 2027

The 2027 design note above allows the L/R calibration *during* totality on the strength of
338 s. This budget prices the overheads it will have to pay: **~25 s per field change** at this
rig's slew-and-settle, and ~6 s to get started after C2. Three field changes inside 338 s would
cost ~75 s of the 338 — still affordable, which is the point, but not free.

**Per-frame readout overhead is not a constant, and should be measured rather than assumed**
when the 2027 ladder is designed. From the cadences above, dead time per frame is 0.19 s at
0.1 s, effectively zero at 0.3 s and 0.6 s (readout overlapping the next exposure), then
0.22–0.37 s again at 1.0–2.0 s. The short-tier penalty is the expensive one: the 0.1 s tier
spent 46 frames to buy 4.6 s of signal and 8.6 s of wall-clock.

The single largest lever on Leon's own numbers was not exposure choice. **Had the sequence
stayed on one field for the whole of totality, the 25.4 s slew spent at the CAL sequence's
74 % duty would have added ~18.8 s of integration — 70.5 s rather than 51.7 s, a duty cycle
of 66 % rather than 48 %.** Against that, the two-field design is what supplies the plate
scale; the trade was made knowingly, and this is its price in seconds.

## The field that was flown was not the field that was designed (2026-09-01)

Douglas' question: was the below-Sun anchor — G 7.71, the single most valuable star in the
field — supposed to be in the frame at all? **No. And the reason is not the mount.**

### The mount hit its mark to under an arcminute

The clean measurement is the plate solve against the mount's own reported pointing, with the
frames reconciled (the solver returns ICRS at the catalogue epoch; `RA`/`DEC` in the header are
JNOW at `EQUINOX` 2026.614, so the two differ by ~0.37° of precession before any pointing error
is counted — that is the whole of the "0.39°" the stage-1 log reports for this field).

Converting the header to ICRS properly: commanded (142.6604, +15.3926), solved
(142.6458, +15.3929) — **a miss of 0.85′, essentially all of it in east–west (−0.85′) with
+0.02′ in Dec**. Whatever else went wrong, the science pointing did not.

*(An earlier draft of this section put the miss at 10 ″ by comparing the Sun-to-frame-centre
distance in pixels against the commanded 0.7371°. That is a scalar and only bounds the radial
component — a perpendicular error is invisible to it. 0.85′ from the plate solve is the number
to quote.)*

### The sensor was 3.4 % larger in angle than the offsets assumed

The canonical plate scale is 2.2054043 ″/px, which at `XPIXSZ` 3.76 µm is a focal length of
**351.7 mm**. The script's offsets were computed for **363.5 mm**. So:

| | assumed (f = 363.5) | actual (f ≈ 351.7) |
|---|---|---|
| frame | 3.704° × 2.475° | **3.828° × 2.558°** |
| half-height | 1.2374° | **1.2791°** |
| up-offset commanded | 0.7374° | 0.7374° (unchanged — the script was not updated) |
| **Sun above the bottom edge** | **0.5000° = 816 px** | **0.5417° = 884 px** |

Measured: the Sun sits **883 px** above the bottom edge. Predicted from the focal-length error
alone: **884 px**. One pixel. The whole 68 px surplus is the focal length, and none of it is
pointing.

### Which is exactly the 68 px the anchor needed

The anchor is at (3161, 4163) — 870 px below the Sun, **13 px from the bottom edge**. Take away
the 68 px of unplanned extra frame and it lands **54 px below the sensor**. Had the script's own
contingency pair been used (`OFFSET 36.5 31.2`, for f = 350.0 mm), the Sun would have sat 0.494°

### The calibration field missed by 1.57° — and it broke the altitude match

matching is frame-to-frame, not frame-to-Sun."* Geometric altitudes at a common instant

|---|---|---|---|
| as designed | 10.4204° | 10.5769° | **+0.156°** |
| **as flown** | **10.4101°** | **9.9580°** | **−0.452°** |

**The match was broken by 0.61°.** The calibration field flew almost half a degree *below* the
science field instead of alongside it. At these altitudes that is not a rounding error:

| | altitude | refraction | gradient dR/dh |
|---|---|---|---|
| science field | 10.410° | 311.5″ | −28.29 ″/deg |
| calibration field | 9.958° | 324.8″ | −30.60 ″/deg |

Refraction differs by **13.3″** and — the term that actually propagates — **the field
compression differs by 8.2 %**. The absolute offset is common-mode and absorbed by the solve;
the gradient is not, because it is what maps a plate scale measured on one field onto the other.
**This belongs in [`REFRACTION_2026.md`](REFRACTION_2026.md)'s error budget and is not currently
in it.** It is not a reason to distrust the 25 ppm plate scale — that is an internal fit to the
calibration field's own stars — but the *transfer* of that scale to the science sightline was
made across a 0.45° altitude step that the design had set to zero, and nobody has priced it.

**Why the two fields differ so much is not explained by any standard mount-model term.** A
single-point solar sync leaves the science field 0.74° away essentially perfect and the
calibration field 9.8° away out by 1.57°; polar misalignment large enough to do that (≈9°) is
absurd, and cone error large enough (≈17°) more so. The residual-drift measurement above is
consistent with only ~0.5–1° of polar misalignment. **This is an open item**, and the honest
statement is that the mount's model degraded far faster with distance from the sync point than
any simple term predicts. For 2027, where the design interleaves L–SCI–R–SCI across totality,
this is the finding that matters most: **every field change is a fresh 1.5°-class pointing
risk, and the altitude match that the refraction scheme depends on cannot be assumed — it has
to be plate-solved and checked, not commanded and trusted.**

### Why the anchor made it in: two errors, and neither was enough on its own

With the Sun's true pixel position in hand (3169, 3242) the margin can be decomposed exactly.
Everything below is in pixels on the actual sensor at the actual plate scale, 2.2054 ″/px; the
anchor's separation from the Sun, **921 px**, is a physical fact and does not move.

| | Sun's y | Sun above bottom edge | anchor at | margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **as flown** | 3242 | 934 px | 4163 | **13 px inside** |
| as designed (correct offset, commanded pointing achieved) | 3360 | 816 px | 4281 | **105 px outside** |

The 118 px of extra margin splits into two independent errors:

| contribution | size | why |
|---|---|---|
| **offset computed for the wrong focal length** | **68 px** | up-offset commanded 0.7374° where f ≈ 351.7 mm needs 0.7791° |
| **pointing / solar drift** | **50 px** | Sun-to-centre came out 1154 px against a commanded 1204 px |

**Neither alone would have put the star on the sensor.** With only the focal-length error the
anchor sits 37 px past the bottom edge; with only the pointing term, 55 px past. It needed both,
and it cleared by 13 px.

So the honest answer to "was it just the plate scale?" is **no — the plate-scale prior is the
larger term at 58 %, but 42 % is pointing, and the star is inside the frame only because the two
happened to push the same way.**

**The 50 px is not all mount error, and part of it is structural.** The science field was placed
by a `MOUNT GOTO SOLARSYSTEM Sun OFFSET …` minutes before C2 and then held under **sidereal**
tracking at the armed prompt. The Sun moves against the stars at ~2.5 ′/hr, so the Sun-to-frame
geometry drifts after the GOTO — of order 0.7 ′ ≈ 19 px along the offset axis over half an hour,
in the direction that *increases* the bottom margin. **The commanded geometry is only true at
the instant of the slew**, and nothing in the script accounts for the wait. The remaining ~30 px
(≈1.1 ′) is pointing and ephemeris residual, consistent with the 0.85 ′ plate-solve miss.

For 2027 this is a design item, not a curiosity: **if a field is placed relative to the Sun and
then held for minutes under sidereal tracking, the offset must be computed for the time the
shutter opens, not the time the mount moves** — or the mount must track solar until the sequence
starts.

### Why the two fields' pointing differed so much — resolved

The previous section logged the science field's 0.85 ′ against the calibration field's 1.57 ° as
unexplained by any standard mount-model term. **Douglas supplies the missing operational fact:
the mount was re-synced on the centre of the Sun a few minutes before totality.** That closes it
without needing an exotic term.

A sync is exact at the sync point and its residual grows with distance from it. The science
field is **0.74 ° from the Sun** and came out 0.85 ′; the calibration field is **9.8 ° away** and
came out 94 ′. Errors of 1.2 ′/° and 9.6 ′/° respectively — the near field is not merely better,
it is better in the way a fresh single-point sync predicts, because the sync absorbs *all* model
error locally and none of it at distance. The earlier arithmetic that made this look impossible
(≈9 ° of polar misalignment required) assumed the model was globally fitted; against a
single-point sync minutes old, no such misalignment is needed.

**The operational consequence is the one that matters for 2027.** A solar sync buys accuracy
that decays with slew distance, so **the calibration field is exactly where the sync is worth
least** — and the calibration field is where the refraction scheme's altitude match has to hold
(broken here by 0.61 °, above). The fix is not a better sync: it is to **plate-solve the
calibration field and correct, rather than trusting the GOTO** — cheap at 338 s of totality,
impossible at 107 s.

## Why the headline L uses the 0.6 s and 1.2 s tiers only (2026-09-01)

Douglas has been asked to justify the choice, and the natural objection is the right one to
answer: the 0.1 s and 0.3 s tiers hold 11.5 s of the ladder's 24.7 s of integration, so why not
use them — with the plate scale borrowed from the deep tiers if they will not solve alone — and
drive the error down with the extra photons? Because that was done, and the record already
contains the measurement. Three facts, all from the S2 union work above.

**1. The short tiers were used, exactly as proposed, and the result is the full-union row.**
The union machinery rides the 0.1 s and 0.3 s frames on the 0.6 s fitted model with a per-tier
constant offset ("solve: inherited" in the per-tier anatomy table) — the borrowed plate scale is
the mechanism, not a suggestion. All four tiers went through the same rematch, the same
cross-tier vet, the same fit. So the comparison below is not "deep tiers vs deep tiers plus an
untested idea"; it is two measured numbers.

| union | N stars | L v-deg2 | ± stat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.6 + 1.2 s (headline) | 42 | **1.98 ″** | 0.60 ″ |
| all four tiers, limit-11 catalogue | 44 | 2.58 ″ | 0.59 ″ |
| all four tiers, after G 9.10 hygiene | 43 | 2.21 ″ | 0.50 ″ |

Adding 11.5 s of integration added **one to two stars** and moved L by **+0.26 to +0.60 ″**
while the statistical error moved by at most 0.1 ″. That is the "combining increases the
error" observation, quantified: the stat error barely changes and a systematic of the size of
the atmosphere term walks in.

**2. Integration is not the resource that limits this measurement, and the ladder proves it
on itself.** The per-star noise, measured from each star's scatter about its own cross-tier
mean, is **0.281 / 0.351 / 0.326 / 0.320 ″ per axis for 0.1 / 0.3 / 0.6 / 1.2 s** — flat across
a 12× range of exposure, where photon statistics would predict a 3.5× spread. The floor is the
quasi-static atmospheric wavefield (M3's ~0.25 ″), coherent over the whole 39 s ladder, and no
exposure averages it down because every frame of every tier sees the same realisation of it.
This is also why inverse-variance weighting across tiers is a measured no-op: the tiers *are*
equally noisy per star, so weighting cannot rescue the full union — the +0.26 ″ is not a
weighting artefact, it is the shallow tiers sampling the wavefield at different moments and
carrying different drift and coronal-subtraction residuals into a per-star median.

So the question "are many short exposures as good as fewer long ones?" has a specific answer
here. In a photon-limited regime they would be nearly as good — read noise is 3.3 e⁻ against
~25 e⁻ of sky noise per 0.1 s frame, so 46 × 0.1 s stacked reaches almost the depth of one
4.6 s frame, and the only intrinsic penalty is the 0.29 s readout per frame (the 0.1 s tier ran
at 35 % duty: 4.6 s of light in 13 s of totality). **But this field is not photon-limited per
star at any tier.** More seconds on the *same* stars buy nothing; the statistical error is set
by the number of stars and their radial leverage h, not by integration.

**3. The short tiers add no stars, because they cannot reach any the deep tiers lack.** Depth
per tier tracks total integration: 0.1 s (4.6 s) reaches V 9.35 with 24 stars; 0.3 s (6.9 s)
V 10.30 with 40; 0.6 s (7.2 s) V 10.30 with 42; 1.2 s (6.0 s) V 9.96 with 38. The 0.1 s and
0.3 s star lists are subsets of the 0.6 s list. Nor did the shallow tiers deliver the thing they
were designed for, the inner annulus: their smaller saturation radius (1.38 / 1.57 R⊙ against
1.71 / 1.86) exposed the steep coronal rim, which fed the solver junk and yielded no star inside
2.17 R⊙ that the 0.6 s tier did not also find (0.1 s: innermost 2.29 R⊙; 0.3 s: 2.17; 0.6 s:
2.17). The 0.1 s tier "retains insurance value only for a V ≲ 7 star inside 1.6 R⊙" — the 2017
configuration, which this field did not have.

**What the objection gets right, and where it points.** Integration *does* buy stars — fainter
ones — and stars are what the error needs. But that argues for *longer* exposures, not for
adding short ones: the mag-12 test found the union detection-limited by the coronal sky against
1.2 s, with nothing fainter than G 10.30 entering, while the 2027 verdict prices a 4.8 s tier at
~0.75 mag of outer-field depth ≈ tens more stars. It also argues for *repeating* the ladder
across totality — the wavefield is quasi-static over minutes, so independent samples of it are
the only way to average the 0.28 ″ floor, and Leon's 39 s ladder is one sample. Both are 2027
design items already recorded above.

**The one test the record does not contain**, stated so nobody mistakes the argument for
complete: a single combined 24.7 s stack of all 87 frames, used for *detection* rather than as
a fifth tier. Sky-limited, it would reach ~0.6 mag deeper than the 7.2 s tiers and might add
stars in V 10.3–11. It was not built because a stack across four saturation radii inherits the
1.2 s frames' 1.86 R⊙ hole and the 0.1 s frames' coronal-rim contamination at once, and because
it would not touch the per-star floor. That is the S0 open item "full-ladder combined stack",
still open; it is a depth experiment, and the clean version of it is the 2027 deep tier.

### What the null-test L is made of, and whether the budget counts anything twice (2026-09-02, late)

Douglas asked what the null-test L actually measures, whether it is a floor for a
particular atmosphere, how it enters the error, whether it averages away with time, whether
Bruns' omission of any such term has been shown wrong — and then, on seeing the first
answers, whether the budget double-counts. Four measurements answer most of it; the rest is
bookkeeping that had not been written down.

#### What the null contains

The null residual is everything the calibration transfer leaves behind between two epochs
or two sightlines, and it has four parts, only two of which are atmosphere:

1. **isotropic plate-scale change** across the gap — the reference's own scale *estimation
   error* (statistical, from its stars) plus the real *drift* between the two epochs
   (thermal, −40 ppm/K on this tube; focus). Enters L at the measured leverage (0.016 ″/ppm
   on the zenith geometry, 0.027 on the eclipse field's, 0.008 on Bruns'). Neither the
   vertical nuisance nor the free offsets and rotation can absorb an isotropic scale;
2. **differential refraction compression** between the two sightlines — a *vertical*
   stretch, not a scale. Enormous at the horizon (the M5 H1-to-H3 free-fit steps are +54,
   +80 and +151 ppm, which at the isotropic leverage would be 0.6–1.5 ″ of L) and mostly
   absorbed by the vertical-deg-2 nuisance, whose v01 term is exactly a linear vertical
   stretch. The ±0.2–0.3 ″ the M5 windows show is what survives it;
3. **the quasi-static wavefield** — patchy, minutes-long, vertically polarised at low
   altitude (V/H 2.4), not absorbed by a smooth nuisance. The only part that is
   "atmosphere" in the everyday sense;
4. **model mismatch** — the frozen cubic is a multi-field average and the field's own cubic
   differs slightly.

Calling the sum "the atmospheric term" is shorthand. Which part dominates depends on the
geometry and the construction, and the way to find out is to refit each null with the
plate scale **free** (Method 2): what collapses was scale-like (part 1), what survives is
shape (parts 2–4).

| null set | pairs | Method 1 null rms | **scale free** null rms | scale-like part (quadrature) | fitted scale step rms |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leon zenith, consecutive, 2.6 min | 10 | 0.124 ″ | **0.061 ″** | 0.108 ″ | 7.2 ppm |
| Bruns one-sided, 6–7 min | 22 | 0.150 ″ | **0.099 ″** | 0.113 ″ | 24.1 ppm |
| Bruns bracketed, 12–13 min span | 16 | 0.087 ″ | **0.086 ″** | 0.01 ″ | 16.2 ppm (fitted, not real: see below) |

Two readings. **The zenith "machinery floor" is mostly plate-scale drift over two and a half
minutes** — half its variance goes away with a free scale, and the fitted step (7.2 ppm) is
the free-fit step between the fields (7.6 ppm). The other half, ±0.06 ″, is shape: model
mismatch and whatever wavefield exists at alt 80°. **Bruns' one-sided null is a third scale
drift too** — his night fields' scales step by 24 ppm between consecutive fields as the tube
cools — **and his bracket removes it completely.** Redone his way, each field against the
mean of the fields before and after it (`tools/matrix_bruns/b17_bracket_null.py`,
`matrix_bruns2017_atmosphere3/bracket_nulls.csv`), the null is ±0.087 ″ and does not move at
all when the scale is freed: what a bracket leaves is pure shape. (The 16.2 ppm "fitted
step" on the bracketed set is the free scale chasing shape it cannot describe, not a real
step — the free-fit scales say the field-against-neighbours'-mean mismatch is 14.8 ppm with
about 5 ppm of that being fit noise, and the null does not correlate with it, r = 0.00.)

#### The double-count question

Three terms, three possible overlaps, each now quantified.

**Stat against the null.** The star-resampling bootstrap measures within-realisation
scatter — incoherent noise plus the sampling of any coherent field by different star
subsets — and cannot see a coherent field that projects onto 1/r the same way for every
star; the null measures the distribution of exactly that bias across realisations. The
overlap is the null's own sampling part, which is its bootstrap floor (0.026–0.037 ″ for the
Bruns and zenith sets, 0.058–0.096 for the M5 windows), and the corrected systematic
√(null² − floor²) differs from the null at the third decimal: 0.150 → 0.145, 0.087 → 0.083,
0.223 → 0.210. The zenith null's 42-star subsample figure (±0.14) is a like-for-like *total*
that overlaps a stat term by about 0.07 ″; quote the full-sample ±0.12 beside a stat term,
never the subsample.

**Scale against the null.** The scale term is the calibration field's own scale
*estimation* error propagated at the measured leverage; the null contains its reference
field's scale estimation error in exactly the same way, so the two overlap by
leverage × σ_S(reference). The reference fields' stored uncertainties settle the size:

| null set | reference fields | σ_S of the reference | leverage | overlap with a scale term |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leon zenith | free cubics, 2 400–2 800 stars | 1.2 ppm | 0.016 ″/ppm | 0.02 ″ |
| Bruns night, one-sided | free cubics, 570–1 300 stars at tol 0.2 | 3.2–4.7 ppm | 0.008 | 0.03 ″ |
| Bruns night, bracketed | mean of two of the above | ~2.8 ppm | 0.008 | 0.02 ″ |
| Leon M5 horizon | H3 stacks, 470–780 stars | 5.4–8.9 ppm | 0.010–0.014 | **0.05–0.10 ″** |

Against the science chain's own scale terms — Bruns' L+R8 at 118 stars each is 10.3 ppm HC3
(0.084 ″), Leon's CAL_piLeo at 74 stars is 25 ppm (0.675 ″) — every null was built on
references far better determined than the calibration the science actually used, so the
overlap is at most 0.1 ″ inside Leon's ±0.33 and 0.03 ″ inside Bruns' ±0.15, worth less than
0.02 ″ to either total in quadrature. The *drift* part of a null (real scale change across
the gap) is not in any scale term and is not double-counted; it is simply mislabelled as
atmosphere, and for Bruns it is charged to a design that cancels it.

**Scale against stat.** None: the bootstrap resamples stars with the scale held at its
imported value.

So the honest answer is: the three-term budget does not count anything twice at a level
that reaches the second decimal of either total. What it does do is over-state cell 1's
atmospheric term by using a one-sided construction for a bracketed design, and label as
"atmosphere" a term that is one-third to one-half plate-scale drift.

#### How it enters the error, and whether it averages away

It is added in quadrature as a systematic, because within one eclipse measurement it is a
fixed pattern: the science stack is 40 s long and the calibration two minutes away, both
inside the quasi-static regime.

The four parts average differently. The frame-to-frame jitter (0.4–0.6 ″ per 6 s frame at
Leon's altitude) averages as √N within a stack and is gone from a 12-frame tier. The
quasi-static wavefield does not average within a 40 s stack — M3 measured it as the median
over 45 frames spanning five minutes and it was still 0.15–0.32 ″ — and it averages only
across *independent realisations*, which a single totality does not supply; 2027's
interleaved L–SCI–R–SCI design is the way to get more than one. The drift and the
compression differential never average: drift *grows* with the calibration-to-science gap
and a bracket cancels its linear part; the compression is fixed by the altitude difference
between the two fields and only a calibration at the science field's own altitude removes
it. So the floor is time-dependent in both directions — longer integration shrinks the
jitter, longer gaps grow the drift — and a campaign's term should name its gap: Leon's ±0.33
is at a 10–13 minute one-sided pairing (over-stating the science chain's own two minutes),
Bruns' ±0.087 is at a bracketed 12-minute span, the zenith ±0.12 is at 2.6 minutes
one-sided.

#### What Bruns did, and whether the assumption has been shown false

Bruns' two error terms are both statistical. His star term (3.1 %) is F&L eq (20) fed the
per-star rms of his eclipse fit — residuals treated as independent per star, which captures
jitter and the incoherent part of the wavefield at star scale. His plate-scale term
(1.23 %) is the formal least-squares uncertainty of the bracket's scale (his rms × √n / Σr
is the OLS standard error of a scale fitted through stars at distances r), which assumes
independent, equal errors; the HC3 estimate on the same fits is 10.3 ppm against his 3.34.
**That factor of three is mostly the INPUT, not the estimator** — see "The plate-scale
error" below: his 0.077 ″ is centroid precision, our 0.21 ″ the actual fit residual (×2.81),
and HC3 against independent-errors OLS adds ×1.51. His figure also omits the √2 of
averaging L and R, which ours takes. He combined the
two in quadrature to 3.4 %. There is no coherent term, and nothing in his text suggests he
considered one: the assumption is implicit — that anything not in the per-star scatter has
averaged away or been cancelled by the bracket.

For his geometry and his design that assumption is *nearly* right, and the bracket is why.
The one-sided nulls say ±0.15 ″ of coherent residual exists at his altitude between fields
minutes apart, but a third of it is the drift his bracket cancels, and what the bracket
leaves is ±0.087 ″ of pure shape. Against a quoted total of 0.060 ″ that is still the
largest single term, so the assumption is false at the ±0.09 ″ level for his data — but it
costs him 0.060 → 0.134 with the three terms, not 0.060 → 0.182. For Leon's geometry the
assumption is simply false: the coherent term is ±0.2–0.3 ″, and a one-sided calibration
half a degree lower in altitude leaves the compression differential uncancelled.

Why his total is smaller than ours even without any atmosphere: our stat term on his data
(±0.060–0.064) *equals* his entire quoted error, and the difference is the plate-scale term —
his formal 3.34 ppm against our HC3 10.3, ±0.021 against ±0.084 ″ of L.

**Proposed, not applied**: cell 1's atmospheric term should be the bracketed ±0.087, the
construction that matches his design, with the one-sided ±0.150 retained in the record as
the analogue of Leon's one-sided CAL. That would make cell 1 **L = 1.764 ± 0.060 (stat)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**The answer to the question is yes, and the mechanism is visible in the means.** Fitting the
eclipse pointing against the RIGHT field alone biases L by −0.039 ″; against the LEFT field
alone by +0.036 ″ — equal and opposite, which is what a smooth gradient across the sky
produces at a point midway between two references. Averaging the two leaves a mean of
−0.001 ″. The bracket is not merely averaging noise: it is cancelling a gradient, and it is
why his design was worth having.

The null falls 0.150 → 0.059, a factor 2.5. Its own bootstrap floor is 0.031, so the
coherent part is **±0.050 ″**.

Three caveats, all in the conservative direction except the last:

* **eight triplets.** The uncertainty on an rms from eight samples is about 25 %, so this is
  ±0.059 ± 0.015;
* **the night bracket is wider than the eclipse-day one.** R-to-L spans 4.2 minutes at night
  against roughly two minutes on eclipse day (R at the opening of totality, the eclipse
  series 17:43:22–17:44:13, L after), and drift grows with span. The night figure over-states;
* **the references here are free cubics**, where the eclipse-day L and R were fitted
  quadratic-free against the frozen 15-field night cubic. A free cubic on 600–1300 stars is
  determined to 3–5 ppm of scale, so the difference is second-order, but it is a difference;
* **these are night fields.** Daytime convective turbulence is generally worse at the same
  altitude, so every one of these numbers is a best case — the caveat the record already
  carries on the ±0.15.

floor, and `tools/floor_vs_sampling.py` answers it from the same residuals the floor table
is built from.

The three instruments differ by a factor of two in plate scale and, from the campaign's own
PSF surveys, by more than that in sampling:

| set | camera | pixel | ″/px | FWHM (px) | FWHM (″) | sampling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leakey 2024 | ASI294MM bin 1 | 2.315 µm | 1.1506 | 2.71 | 3.12 | adequate |
| Leon 2026 | ASI2600MM | 3.76 µm | 2.2054 | 2.58 | 5.69 | adequate |
| Bruns 2017 | FLI ML8051 | 5.50 µm | 2.0868 | **1.64** | 3.42 | **undersampled** |

(Leon's FWHM is `psf_portland` — the same FRA500 + 0.7× + ASI2600 rig on a different night,
so it is indicative rather than Leon's own.)

**The separation is done by magnitude, because the two candidate causes behave oppositely.**
Centroid noise scales as FWHM/SNR and is magnitude-dependent; structure — model mismatch,
drift, atmosphere — displaces a bright star and a faint star by the same angle and is
magnitude-independent. So binning the quadratic-free residuals in magnitude splits them:

| set | G 4–8 | 8–9 | 9–10 | 10–11 | 11–12 | 12–13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leakey 2024 zenith | 0.133 | 0.077 | 0.067 | 0.064 | 0.070 | 0.102 |
| Leon 2026 zenith | 0.086 | 0.070 | 0.066 | 0.065 | 0.063 | 0.069 |
| Bruns 2017 night | 0.272 | **0.047** | 0.058 | 0.103 | 0.125 | **0.176** |

| set | structure floor, G 8–10 | in pixels | G 11–13 | in pixels | faint/bright | G ≤ 11 (the table) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leakey 2024 | **0.072 ″** | 0.063 px | 0.086 | 0.075 | 1.19 | 0.078 |
| Leon 2026 | **0.068 ″** | 0.031 px | 0.066 | 0.030 | **0.97** | 0.067 |
| Bruns 2017 | **0.052 ″** | 0.025 px | 0.150 | 0.072 | **2.88** | 0.100 |

**Sampling does not set the floor, and the pixel column is how you can tell.** At the bright
end the three floors agree to a factor **1.4 in angle** (0.052–0.072 ″) but spread by a factor
**2.5 in pixels** (0.025–0.063 px). A floor that were a centroiding or sampling artefact
would be the constant one in pixels; it is the constant one in arcseconds. Leakey's 1.9×
finer sampling buys it nothing — its floor is the *largest* of the three in angle and 2.5×
Bruns' in pixels, which is exactly what a fixed angular floor looks like when the pixels
shrink. The floor does not track FWHM either: Leakey has the sharpest PSF in arcseconds
(3.12 ″) and the highest floor, Leon the worst (5.69 ″) and a middling one.

**Where sampling and aperture do enter is the faint end, and it is Bruns who pays.** His
faint/bright ratio is **2.88** against Leon's 0.97 — Leon's residuals are flat from G 8 to
G 13, so his floor contains no visible photon noise at all, while Bruns' rise from 0.047 ″
at G 8–9 to 0.176 ″ at G 12–13. That is consistent with his being the one undersampled
instrument (1.64 px FWHM, below Nyquist) on the smallest aperture at a short exposure.

**The consequence matters for the floor table, and it reverses an ordering.** The table
compares at G ≤ 11, the science cut, and at that cut Bruns' number is roughly half his own
faint-star photon noise: 0.100 ″ against a structure floor of 0.052 ″. Leon's is unaffected
(0.067 against 0.068). So the G ≤ 11 ordering — Leon 0.067 < Leakey 0.078 < Bruns 0.100 —
is **not** a structure ordering. At the bright end it reverses to **Bruns 0.052 < Leon 0.068
≈ Leakey 0.072**: his instrument and model are the *cleanest* of the three, not the worst,
and the earlier reading that his night fields "are barely atmospheric at all" is
strengthened rather than weakened.

Two things this does not change. The nulls already price each instrument's own photon noise
separately, in their bootstrap floors — 0.037 for Bruns against 0.025 for Leon, the ratio
1.5 being exactly his noisier faint stars — so removing it leaves Bruns' one-sided null at
0.145 ″ against the zenith 0.121 ″, and the pedestal argument stands. And the table keeps
the G ≤ 11 cut, because that is the cut the eclipse budgets are computed at; the bright-end
column is the diagnostic beside it, not a replacement.

**For 2027 there are two separable levers here, and they are not the obvious ones.** Finer
sampling does not lower the floor — Leakey proves that directly. What lowers it is focus
stability (the refocus experiment: 0.094 → 0.051 ″) and calibration cadence (the floor is
two-thirds plate-scale drift). What finer sampling *does* buy is faint-star precision, and
therefore the ability to push the magnitude cut deeper without importing noise — which is
worth having for a different reason, since more stars is how the statistical term falls.

### The plate-scale error: whose estimate counts the two fields, and what Leon's one-sided calibration cost (2026-09-02, last)

Two questions from Douglas, both answerable by running his own formula
(`tools/matrix_bruns/b17_scale_error_audit.py`).

#### His formula, verified, and what it does and does not include

He states it in words: "the RMS error multiplied by the square root of the number of stars,
divided by the sum of their distances from the image center" — that is

    σ_S/S  =  rms · √n / Σ|r_i|

the ordinary least-squares standard error of a scale fitted through stars at distances r_i
under independent, equal-variance errors. Fed **his** rms of 0.077 ″ and our star geometry
it returns **3.11–3.14 ppm** against his stated 3.34, so the transcription is right and his
arithmetic checks out. His own sanity check confirms the reading: taking "mean centroid
error" as the standard error of the mean (rms/√n) and "widest separation" as the sensor's
long side, the simple form gives 2.29 ppm against the detailed 3.13, a factor **1.36–1.37**
where he says "closer to 1.5".

**The two-field averaging is not in his number.** He feeds the formula one rms — "the RIGHT
and LEFT master images have a *mean* RMS centroid error of 0.077 arcsec" — and one star
count, 96. Our reductions of those same two fields find **126 and 124 stars each**, so 96 is
one field's worth, not the pooled 250. Since his σ falls as 1/√n, pooling both fields' stars
would have carried the √2 automatically; using one field's does not. Had he applied it his
figure would be **2.36 ppm**, his scale term 1.23 % → 0.87 %, and his total 3.4 % → 3.2 %.
Immaterial to him, because his star term dominates — but it means his stated plate-scale
error is conservative by √2, not optimistic.

**It is in ours.** The per-field HC3 on those fits is 14.5 and 14.7 ppm, and the record's
bracket figure of 10.3 ppm is 14.6/√2 (`docs/STEPS12_LEON_VS_BRUNS2017.md`, step 2). So the
averaging of L and R is already taken.

from the **windowed** L/R pair (105/110 stars, HC0 13.4/13.6), while the reduction of record
uses the Gaussian + moments pair (126/124 stars, HC0 11.9/12.6 → HC3 ≈ 12.9/13.6 → bracket
**9.2 ppm**). Carrying 10.3 where 9.2 applies makes cell 1's scale term 0.084 ″ where 0.075 ″
would be consistent, and the total 0.119 ″ where 0.113 ″ would be. ~~Left as it stands — it is
conservative, and re-deriving a headline for 0.006 ″ is not worth the churn~~ — but the next
cell should take its scale term from its own convention of record.

#### So what is the factor of three between 3.34 and 10.3 ppm?

Not what the record said. Running his formula on our tables separates the two candidates:

| | per field | ratio |
|---|---|---|
| his formula, his rms (0.077 ″ centroid precision) | 3.13 ppm | — |
| his formula, **our** rms (0.21–0.22 ″ fit residual) | 8.78 ppm | **×2.81 — the input** |
| our HC3 | 13.27 ppm | **×1.51 — the estimator** |

**The dominant cause is the input, not the estimator.** His 0.077 ″ is a *centroid-precision*
figure; our 0.21 ″ is the actual *fit residual*, which also contains catalogue error,
distortion-model mismatch and atmosphere. Propagating centroid precision alone assumes the
model is perfect and the catalogue exact; propagating the residual asks how much the fitted
scale could move given how the stars actually scatter about the model, whatever the cause.
The estimator (HC3 against independent-errors OLS) contributes a further 1.51.

Per field the honest ratio is therefore **4.24 = 2.81 × 1.51**. The quoted ratio looks like
three only because ours carries the √2 of averaging and his does not.

> calibration residuals". That names the ×1.51 and misses the ×2.81. The correlation is
> real and is the smaller half; the larger half is that the two figures propagate different

   residual on the null (0.063″ against 0.073–0.077″).
2. **The background matters only for the moments.** Gaussian halves the moment bias
   (+16 → +5–16, field-dependent) and does nothing for the windowed estimator — Bruns'
   lever, at his 1.64 px FWHM, has no purchase at 3.7 px. Annular and Gaussian are
   equivalent under windowed; annular keeps the two-witness pipeline's default.
3. **The 2024 quintics carry twice the bias of a modern moment fit.** The σ_sub 3
   footprint subtraction (subtracting 3 σ from every pixel before the moment) is what
   doubled it: +26/+32 in 2024 against +13/+17 now on the same frames. The frozen quintic
   the eclipse imports is the 2024 one, so the calibration side of the transfer carries
   this — bounded at 0.011″ in L by the injection (§"Cell 2 opened"), but re-derivable
   windowed from these two fields, and that is the next step (below).

#### One anomaly, run down and harmless

Field 1's stacks carry 15–17 thousand centroids against field 2's 7–8 thousand. The raw
frames are identical in level (603 ADU in every quadrant of every frame, no gradient, no
cloud), the extra detections are spread uniformly, and sampling the nineteen raw frames at
their positions shows them to be 4 σ noise — median excess 1.3 ADU, bright in 0–3 random
frames, never persistent (a warm-pixel population would be bright in ≥ 17 of 19; 0.0 % are).
They falsely match G 14–15 catalogue stars (field 1's G 14–15.5 rms is 0.76–1.4″ against
field 2's 0.2″, and its tangential slope in the raw table is +16–32 mas/mag from that bin
alone), do not move the fitted scale (refitting on G ≤ 13 changes it by < 3 ppm), and do not
touch anything at G ≤ 13. Why field 1's stack yields three times as many is not
identified — its alignment shifts are 0–3 px against field 2's 0–10 — and on the eclipse field
the two-witness rule removes such detections by construction. The 2024 min_area 4 excluded
them.

propagate through the model; it acts on the science field's own centroids, which is the other
side.

**Why the eclipse field's bias is an order of magnitude larger than the zenith field's.**
Comparing the two stacks star by star, the windowed-minus-moments centroid difference is
strongly magnitude-dependent: **+86 mas at G 5–10, −193 mas at G 10–11, −325 mas at G 11–12**.

gradient, and the residual after an annular subtraction scales inversely with the star's own
flux, so the bias grows toward the faint end far faster than the coma-driven bias of a dark
zenith field. A Gaussian window down-weights exactly the wings where that residual lives.

#### 4. Darks and flats for Mexico 2024

`docs/CALIBRATION_FRAMES.md` is careful about what was established for the other cells:
flat-darks and the 2026 CAL_piLeo calibration "not needed" (measured), zenith calibration

measurements are all at −20 °C or +10 °C and do not cover campaigns where dark current is
measurable.

Station 1 is the first cell where that caveat could bite, and the first where the flagged
eclipse-field risk can be tested. Measured in `docs/CALIBRATION_FRAMES.md` §"Measured: Mexico
2024"; the short version:

| | verdict | why |
|---|---|---|
| flat | **not needed** | 2.4 mas rms (footprint moment) / 3.3 mas (windowed) by injecting a PSF of the measured FWHM 3.74 px through the real master flat at 4000 positions, against a 47–56 mas per-star residual. A quintic removes almost none of it, so it is pixel-scale PRNU, random per star, not a bias. |
| dark, as a pedestal | not needed | the 400 ms master's median excess over bias is **0.00 ADU** even at 25 °C, so the 35 °C mismatch against the −10 °C lights is immaterial at this exposure |
| **dark, as a hot-pixel map** | **needed** | the hot pixels are real at −10 °C, they stack coherently, and the dark-free search has no leverage here |

The third row is the one that differs from every other cell, and it is the risk that document
flagged rather than either of the usual worries. Three facts make it:

* **The hot pixels are there.** At the 93 sites the +25 °C master calls hot, the eclipse
  frames themselves sit **+41.5 ADU** above a local background — 3.3 σ of a single frame,
  44 % of them above 5 σ against 5.4 % at random. They run at about **1/30** the amplitude
  the warm dark predicts, which is the temperature difference doing its work.
* **They stack coherently**, because the field is well tracked. Stars in two opposite corner
  windows move within ±0.25 px over all 123 frames, so a hot pixel stays on its pixel and a
  41 ADU excess against a 12.6 ADU per-frame noise reaches roughly **37 σ** on the stack, far
  above the 4 σ detection threshold.
* **The dark-free fallback cannot help.** It ran — `hotpixels.dither_span` is the largest
  *pairwise* offset, and about five frames the aligner placed 3–4 px out (they matched 18–25
  stars against a typical 30–31) carried it to 5.39 px, past the 3 px gate. On a sub-pixel
  field that is the regime the gate exists to prevent, and it found 0 hot pixels from four
  bright candidates on a 61-megapixel frame. Not evidence of absence.

The temperature mismatch does **not** disqualify the darks for this use, because the pipeline
*excludes* dark-flagged pixels from the stack rather than subtracting them: it needs to know
which pixels, not how much. All four eclipse tiers have an exactly matching dark
(250 / 300 / 400 ms against 0.25 / 0.3 / 0.4 / 0.3 s).

**So: use `--dark` on the eclipse re-stack, skip the flat.** Expect and accept the library's
warning about the 35 °C setpoint difference.

> Two corrections came out of this, both recorded in `CALIBRATION_FRAMES.md`. A phase
> correlation is not usable for measuring dither on an eclipse field — vignetting times a
> bright corona is high-contrast and fixed to the detector, so it returns zero however the
> field moves, with a healthy-looking peak. And the first version of that section said the
> dark-free search declined; it ran. The robustness of `dither_span` against a few mis-solved
> frames, and the aligner's silent 3–4 px errors on degraded frames, are filed as separate
> work.

#### 3 and 4. "Achromatic" is retired as a term, and here is the correct name

Calling this "the magnitude-independence test" (in `s1_estimator_arbiter.py` and its commit) was wrong,

qualified**: it is deceptive here twice over. Deflection is indeed independent of wavelength,
but nothing in the test varies wavelength — and these campaigns shoot through a **red filter**
(`observation_wavelength` 0.62–0.65 µm), so the data are essentially monochromatic and carry no
chromatic leverage to test even in principle.

What the test varies is **apparent magnitude**. The physical fact it leans on is that the
deflection depends only on the light ray's impact parameter: a faint star and a bright star at
the same angular distance from the Sun are bent by the same angle. It is the
**magnitude-independence** test.

Mechanically: fit the deflection term as `L + Lmag·(G − 10)` instead of `L`, and read `Lmag`
in arcseconds of apparent deflection per magnitude. Gravity gives zero. A non-zero value is
the estimator, not the Sun.

### The red filter and the cloud, and a flux measurement corrected (2026-09-03, last)

Douglas: "this is perhaps because most of the stars are much more red than the sun and we are
using a red filter. That was the purpose of the red filter, to remove the blue scattered
skylight to improve contrast with stars that tend more towards the red. But a cloud is
generally white, not blue, thereby raising the sky brightness in the red part of the spectrum
disproportionately more. Does that make sense?"

**Yes, and the physics is straightforward.** Clear-sky brightness is Rayleigh scattering off
air molecules, which goes as λ⁻⁴ and is therefore strongly blue; a red filter cuts most of it.
Cloud brightness is Mie scattering off water droplets far larger than the wavelength, which is
nearly grey; a red filter cuts almost none of it. So the filter lowers the clear-sky baseline
without lowering the cloud's contribution, and **the same cloud produces a larger *fractional*
sky rise through a red filter than it would unfiltered.** The operational corollary is worth
stating: a red filter buys contrast against the clear sky and buys nothing against cloud, so a
campaign that relies on it is relatively *more* exposed to cloud, not less.

Single-band data cannot test the colour argument directly — there is no second filter to
compare — and the frame headers record no filter keyword at all (`BAYERPAT` NONE, `COLORTYP`
RAW16, nothing else), so the red filter is taken from Douglas' account.

#### A correction: the stars dim by 14 %, they do not brighten by 4 %

The previous entry reported stellar flux **4 % higher** in the cloudy frames and treated the
absence of extinction as a puzzle. That measurement was wrong. It took the local background as
the **median of a 41 px box**, and under the steep, moving gradient these frames carry, a box
median sits below the true background on the brighter side and the star's summed flux is
inflated. Redone with an **annulus** at 9–14 px, which follows a gradient far better:

| | calm frames | cloudy frames | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| stellar flux (annular background) | 1.047 | 0.900 | **−14.1 %** |
| sky level | 678 | 819 ADU/s | +20.8 % |

So the stars **do** dim, and the puzzle dissolves. Extinction of 14 % is an optical depth of
τ ≈ 0.15 in the beam, alongside a 21 % rise in scattered sky — cloud in or near the line of
sight, doing both of the things cloud does.

#### What that does to the position-scatter answer

It also revises the previous entry's conclusion, which leaned on there being no S/N loss.
A centroid on a bright coronal background is background-limited, so

    σ_centroid  ∝  σ_PSF · √sky / flux  =  √1.21 / 0.859  =  **1.28**

The flux and sky changes alone therefore predict a **+28 %** rise in per-frame position
scatter. Set against the two measurements:

| | scatter ratio, cloudy / calm |
|---|---|
| raw split at the median tilt | 1.68 |
| **predicted from the measured flux and sky** | **1.28** |
| split after detrending both series against frame number | 1.06 |

**The detrended 1.06 is a lower bound, not the answer.** If the cloud arrived and thickened
monotonically through the sixty-second block — which the rising sky and falling flux say it
did — then the time trend *is* the cloud effect, and detrending removes the signal along with
the confound. The raw 1.68 is an upper bound for the opposite reason. The physically anchored
+28 % sits between them and is the number to carry.

That supersedes the previous entry's "the cloud-specific effect is a few per cent". The
correct statement is that **cloud degrades the per-frame centroid precision by roughly a
quarter to a third through ordinary extinction and sky, and there is no evidence it moves
stars systematically** — the brightness-independence of the scatter increase (1.76 against
1.79 for the bright and faint halves) still rules out a background-gradient pull, and that
result is unaffected, because a pure S/N degradation is multiplicative and therefore
brightness-independent in exactly this way.

Since the effect is noise rather than bias, it averages down in a 123-frame stack. It argues
for frame weighting or a frame-quality cut rather than for any correction to L.

#### One test that could not be made cleanly

Whether the cloud is *in the beam* or off-axis scattering sunlight in would be settled by
asking, within a frame, whether stars under a brighter local sky are dimmer. The per-frame
correlation is +0.08 overall and −0.24 in the cloudiest quartile — the right sign when it
matters, but the test is confounded: the local sky at these fourteen positions spans 567 to
4672 ADU/s, and almost all of that range is the **corona**, not cloud. The extinction
measurement above answers the question anyway: 14 % of the starlight is gone, so something is
in the beam.

#### The mismatch is governed by signal-to-noise, not by sky brightness

background at its 185 matched stars spans a factor of 3.3 — so the question can be asked

**So the sky is not the variable.** What the moment estimator's bias tracks is the star's own

estimator's equivalent is near zero in both. Keeping moments on both sides does not protect the
transfer, because the bias is not common to the two fields — it is precisely what differs.

The bottom-right panel of `Error_graphs.png` is the per-star fit residual against distance

causes and they need separating, because two are expected and one has a real cost.

| output | fit type | residual r < 2000 | r > 4000 | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the chart named | constant-only, corrections flags **mismatched** | 0.454 ″ | 1.445 ″ | 3.18 |
| eclipse blocks | constant-only, scale −600 ppm | 2.15 ″ | 5.10 ″ | 2.3 |

#### 1. The chart named is a bug already found and fixed

That output is from `zenith_nulls_test`, the very first exploratory refit of this campaign,
run **before** the corrections flags were matched to the 2024 quintics. The 2024 fits were
made against apparent places with refraction and aberration on; refitting with them off leaves
**−163 ppm** of scale, and a scale error is *exactly* a linear ramp: 163 ppm reaches **1.74 ″
at the corner**, against the 1.44 ″ measured. That whole tree is superseded by
`zenith_nulls_corr`, and the trap is recorded under §"Cell 2 opened".

#### 2. In any constant-only fit a scale mismatch must show as a ramp

This is by construction, not a defect: `distortion_fixed_coefficients=constant` imports the
scale and refuses to refit it, so whatever scale difference exists appears as a displacement
growing linearly from the frame centre. The eclipse blocks sit **−600 ppm** from their zenith
calibration because of the daytime refocus, which is 6.4 ″ at the corner — and that *is* the
~4 ″ stage-2 rms those fits report. Method 2 fits the scale at stage 3, so none of it reaches
L.

**But it has one real cost, and this is the part that is a bad sign.** A stage-2 residual of
several arcseconds forces `distortion_fit_tol = 20 ″`, and a 20 ″ tolerance is what admitted
the G 10.2 "close-in star" at 1.87 R☉ whose displacement was −15.9 ″. The ramp did not bias
the fit; it forced a loose gate that let a mis-identification through. **The fix is a
scale-corrected pre-match** — match with the scale free, then refit constant-only on that star
set, so the matching gets a tight tolerance while the fit still refuses to absorb deflection.
Filed as the next methodological change.

#### 3. The free fits have a mild ramp, and it is noise rather than model error

The seventeen free quintics — the reference of record — run 1.18 to 1.44 from centre to edge.
Three candidate causes, all tested:

* **Under-fitting?** No. Refitting two fields at septic order improves the rms by 5 % but does
  so mostly at the *centre* (0.099 → 0.085 ″) and barely at the edge (0.124 → 0.117 ″), so the
  ratio gets *worse*, 1.25 → 1.38. A higher order does not flatten it.
* **Defocus or coma at the edge?** No, and the sign is the opposite of the guess. The PSF on
  the zenith stack runs **7.55 px FWHM at the centre and 5.26 px beyond 5000 px** — it gets
  *sharper* outward, and its elongation beyond 4000 px is tangential (2.32 px radial against
  2.50 tangential), not radial.
* **A coherent model error?** No. Pooling all seventeen fields, the **mean** radial residual
  oscillates about zero with no trend — −32, −46, +15, +24, −33, +55 mas across the six radius
  bins — while the **rms** grows 81 → 115 mas. The tangential mean is within ±2 mas
  everywhere, and the rms grows almost identically (73 → 112 mas). An un-modelled radial
  distortion would be radial-dominated and would show in the mean; this is **isotropic scatter
  with a mean of zero**.

So it is a signal-to-noise gradient. Vignetting is part of it: the flat falls 22.8 % to the
corner, and since it cuts star and sky alike, a background-limited centroid degrades as
1/√v = 1.06 beyond 4000 px. That accounts for about a fifth of the measured 1.28. The
remainder is not identified, and is small enough that it belongs in the error budget rather
than in a correction — which is where it already is, since these residuals are what the
budget is built from.

**And it explains why flat-fielding cannot help.** A flat rescales the measured value but the
photons are already lost, so the noise stays; meanwhile dividing the corners by 0.75–0.90
amplifies the noise that is there. That is exactly what the flat test measured: 3–7 % fewer
stars and a 5–7 % *worse* fit residual.

#### Verdict

A ramp is a bad sign only in the constant-only eclipse fits, and there the problem is not the
ramp itself but the loose match tolerance it forces. In the free fits it is a benign
signal-to-noise gradient already carried in the quoted errors.

| warming | tube grows | plate scale |
|---|---|---|
| +5 K | 84 µm | **+199 ppm** |
| +10 K | 167 µm | +399 ppm |
| +15 K | 251 µm | +598 ppm |

#### 2. The pooled fit: every observation its own row, one shared L

Douglas' suggestion, and it is the better model. Instead of averaging each star's residual
over the blocks that saw it and fitting ~140 rows, every block observation is its own row —
~540 of them — and the fit carries **a separate offset, rotation and scale for each block**
with only L shared. That is right for blocks shot minutes apart under changing cloud, and it
is exactly the block-specific nuisance the union could not express.

| | rows | stars | L | ± formal | ± star bootstrap | residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| union, three-witness (residuals averaged per star) | 139 | 139 | 1.987 | 0.105 | — | 0.142 ″ |
| **pooled, stars seen in ≥ 3 blocks, one vet pass** | **541** | **142** | **1.869** | 0.077 | **0.098** | 0.201 ″ |

Two things to read carefully. The formal ±0.077 treats 541 rows as independent, and they are
not: the four observations of one star share its catalogue position and its place in the
distortion model. A bootstrap that resamples **whole stars** with all their observations gives
**±0.098**, and that is the honest figure. And the pooled L sits 0.12 ″ below the union's:
that is the block-specific scales doing their work, absorbing block-to-block differences the
union had folded into the per-star residuals. The pooled residual is per observation, so it is
naturally larger than the union's per-star-averaged 0.142 and is not a worse fit.

**Cell 2's central value on the corona-subtracted, three-of-four, windowed reduction is
therefore L = 1.87 ± 0.10 ″ (statistical), from the pooled fit.** This supersedes the union
figure as the estimator of record; the union stays as a cross-check.

### The two-pass match: the stage-2 gate fixed in the pipeline, and it moves nothing (2026-09-04, last)

Douglas confirmed the tolerance as a flaw of the stage-2 pipeline, and it is closed.

**The flaw.** With the fixed order `constant`, stage 2 fits the offset and rotation of the
field and *imports* the reference files' plate scale. On Station 1 the only reference is the
zenith calibration, and the daytime refocus put the eclipse −594 ppm from it, so the imported
scale is wrong by 5–6 ″ at the corners of a 9576 px frame. That forced the 20 ″ fit tolerance,
which is also the gate on which matched stars survive, and a 20 ″ gate admits a mis-match near
the Sun (the 1.87 R☉ star, 15.9 ″ off, in §"The other exposures"). Every eclipse stage 2 in this
record reported a ~4 ″ rms for the same reason — the scale error, not the stars.

**The fix, two options in the pipeline, both off by default** (`mee2024/config.py`,
`distortion_polynomial.do_cubic_fit`, `distortion_fitter.match_and_fit_distortion`):

* `distortion_free_scale` — with the higher orders still fixed from the reference, keep the
  plate scale the stage-2 linear fit finds instead of overwriting it with the reference's.
  The stretch and skew are discarded exactly as before; only the isotropic scale is kept, and
  the results file now says whether the scale was "imported from the reference files" or
  "fitted on this field".
* `distortion_fit_tol_initial` — a coarse gate for the first fit; `distortion_fit_tol` is then
  applied to the residuals of the *refitted* solution and the fit repeated. This second option
  exists because the first one alone failed: a single 3 ″ gate with the scale free kept **27
  of 189 stars** on the 0.4 s block. The first fit is made on everything the 100 ″ rough match
  admitted, and seven gross mis-assignments pulled its rms to 13.3 ″, so a 3 ″ gate applied to
  *that* fit's residuals threw out the good stars with the bad. Coarse gate, refit, fine gate,
  refit:

| block | pre-gate rms | 20 ″ gate removed | refit rms | 3 ″ gate removed | final rms, stars to G 13 | fitted scale ″/px |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 s | 13.5 ″ | 9 | 0.643 ″ | 1 | **0.499 ″**, 168 | 1.8472864 |
| 0.3 s | 20.7 ″ | 16 | 0.601 ″ | 1 | **0.464 ″**, 178 | 1.8472837 |
| 0.4 s | 13.3 ″ | 7 | 0.564 ″ | 1 | **0.445 ″**, 181 | 1.8472823 |
| 0.3 s | 6.7 ″ | 3 | 0.578 ″ | 1 | **0.460 ″**, 184 | 1.8472826 |

The stage-2 rms falls from ~4 ″ to 0.45–0.50 ″, and the four fitted scales agree with each
other to 2 ppm. They are *not* the published plate scale and must not be compared with it: a
scale fitted without L has the deflection absorbed into it, −46 ppm here. The comparable
number is the joint scale — stage 2's scale corrected by the S_b the pooled fit finds
alongside L — **1.847366–1.847370 ″/px across the four blocks, +2 to +4 ppm from the
1.847363 ± 0.000013 ″/px of Dittrich et al. 2025**, inside its 7 ppm. (An earlier line here
compared the stage-2 scales with 1.8472832 and called it the published value. It is not; it
was a deflection-absorbed figure carried in this record, which is why they agreed.) The star
the 3 ″ gate removes is the same one in all four blocks: G 13.0 at 11 R☉, 5 ″ off — outside the
science set in any case. The 61 ″ mis-match at 2.24 R☉ in the first 0.3 s block was beyond both

**Why fitting the scale at stage 2 is legitimate under Method 2.** The objection to a free
scale at stage 2 was that it absorbs deflection. It does — and the deflection fit refits the
scale jointly with L on the stage-2 residuals, so whatever stage 2 absorbed it gives back: two
linear least-squares steps whose second contains the first's columns are one joint fit
(Frisch–Waugh–Lovell). Measured, not assumed: the pooled fit on the two-pass tables finds
per-block scales of −46 to −50 ppm where the imported-scale tables gave +591 to +594 ppm —
the −48 ppm is exactly the deflection that stage 2's scale had absorbed, handed back — and
**L = 1.871 ± 0.100 ″ against 1.869 ± 0.100 ″**, on the same 541 observations of the same
142 stars, with the same 0.201 ″ residual.

**Verdict.** The gate was a flaw and is fixed; on this data it had admitted nothing the
science cuts (G ≤ 12, 2–9 R☉, three of four blocks) and the vet were not already removing,
and L moves +0.002 ″. The two-pass tables (`eclipse_corona/<tag>/stage2_twopass/`) are the
stage-2 of record from here, because they are the correct reduction and because their 0.45 ″
rms is a number a reader can interpret. Classification: additive, both options off by
default, so no existing run changes; 137 tests in the touched modules pass, three new ones
cover the free scale (`tests/test_distortion_polynomial.py`), and the full suite was run.

### The pooled fit, explained (Douglas' question), and now a tool

`tools/matrix_station1/s1_pooled_fit.py` is the estimator of record, replacing the inline
script. For a star *i* seen in block *b* the stage-2 residual (observed − catalogue, after the
seventeen-field quintic and the constant-only fit) is modelled as

    dx_ib = x0_b − θ_b·y_i + S_b·x_i + L·(R☉/r_i)·ux_i
    dy_ib = y0_b + θ_b·x_i + S_b·y_i + L·(R☉/r_i)·uy_i

with (x_i, y_i) the position from the frame centre in arcseconds, r_i the distance from the
Sun, (ux_i, uy_i) the unit vector away from it, and R☉ the solar radius at the block's
mid-time. Each block carries its own offset, rotation and scale — four nuisance parameters —
and every block shares one L: 17 parameters, 583 observations, 1166 equations. It is a single
least-squares solve, not an average of four block results, so a block with more stars weighs
more and a star seen in four blocks weighs four times a star seen once. Admission needs no
rule — every observation in the science set is a row; the next section measures what the old
rules would do — and the vet removes observations beyond median + 4 MAD once and refits
(explained there).

Why a scale per block and not one: the blocks were shot over three minutes under moving
cloud and each is its own stack. The answer is in the fitted values — the four S_b agree to
4 ppm (591.3, 594.3, 592.0, 590.5 ppm on the imported-scale tables) — so the freedom costs
almost nothing, and it is what the union estimator (residuals averaged per star, one scale)
could not express; the union's L sat 0.12 ″ higher for that reason. Each block alone on the
same rows gives 1.98, 1.72, 1.78, 1.82 ″: the pooled 1.82 is their star-weighted combination.

### The flat is not a lever: it is re-drawn measurement noise, and the 0.3 ″ was an artefact (2026-09-04, last)

The section above reported that flat-fielding moves L by −0.29 ″ on identical stars and called
it the cell's largest unexplained sensitivity. **That is withdrawn.** Douglas asked for the
quick test; it is `s1_flat_mechanism.py`, it takes a minute, and it says the effect is noise.

**1. The pixel response is 1.4 mas, computed rather than injected.** A smooth multiplicative
gradient across a PSF shifts a windowed centroid by about σ_psf²·d(ln F)/dx. Sampled from the
master flat at all 144 stars: |d ln F/dx| is 7 × 10⁻⁵ per pixel at the median, 4 × 10⁻⁴ at the
worst, giving **1.4 mas median and 7.7 mas at the worst star**. That confirms the injection
test and rules the pixel response out as the cause of a 250 mas shift.

**2. The shift tracks brightness and nothing else.** Spearman ρ of |shift| against:

| against | flat − neither | dark − neither |
|---|---|---|
| **G magnitude (fainter = larger)** | **+0.70** | **+0.78** |
| local flat value | +0.04 | |
| \|grad ln F\| at the star | −0.07 | −0.06 |
| the predicted pixel-response shift | −0.07 | |
| distance from the Sun | −0.04 | |
| distance from the frame centre | −0.02 | |

| brightness quartile | G 4.0–9.0 | G 9.0–9.8 | G 9.9–10.5 | G 10.5–11.4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \|shift\|, flat − neither | 56 mas | 220 mas | 368 mas | **461 mas** |

The bright quartile barely moves; the faint quartile moves eight times as far. **That is the
signature of measurement noise, not of a displacement field.** A field-structured systematic
would not know a star's magnitude, and it would move neighbours together: the nearest-neighbour
correlation of the shift is **+0.08** (flat) and +0.04 (dark), at a median separation of 310 px.
There is no field.

**3. It is not the aligner.** All four arms align to the same 123 frames within 0.004 px of
spread (y 0.302–0.306, x 0.993–0.997, span 5.37–5.38 px). The stacks differ in their pixels,
not in where the frames were put.

**4. And ΔL is exactly what that noise predicts.** The arms share their photons, so the right
null keeps each star's shift *amplitude*, randomises its *direction*, adds it to the base arm
and refits:

| arm | measured ΔL | isotropic-noise prediction | |
|---|---|---|---|
| dark only | +0.023 ″ | ±0.035 ″ | 0.6 σ |
| flat only | +0.085 ″ | ±0.308 ″ | 0.3 σ |
| dark + flat | +0.159 ″ | ±0.287 ″ | 0.6 σ |

**Where the −0.29 ″ came from.** It was measured on "the 138 stars in every arm's vetted
science set" — a set defined by *each arm's own vet*, so the arms were compared on stars
selected differently in each. On the 144-star common science set with no vet at all:

| arm | L, 144 common stars, no vet | L, 138 arm-vetted stars |
|---|---|---|
| neither | 1.662 ± 0.239 | 1.974 |
| dark only | 1.684 | 2.012 |
| flat only | 1.746 | 1.688 |
| dark + flat | 1.820 | 1.811 |

Six stars, removed by one arm's vet and not another's, move `neither` by +0.31 ″ and
`flat only` by −0.06 ″, and the gap between them is the whole "lever". On the common science
set the four arms span **0.16 ″ with ±0.23 ″ on each**, which is agreement.

**What stands.** The dark still removes every hot-pixel detection and is still worth having for
that. The calibration choice still contributes per-star noise — the flat arm's centroids are
not the same measurement as the un-flatted arm's, and the fainter the star the less they agree,
which is worth understanding at the pixel level one day. But **there is no 0.2–0.4 ″ systematic
in L, nothing to carry in the budget, and the "largest unexplained sensitivity in cell 2" was a
subset-selection artefact of my own making.** The lesson is the one the vet always teaches:
never compare two reductions on stars that each reduction chose for itself.

### The plate scale changed because the system has a reducer (Douglas' question)

Douglas: both sessions were focused on the stars they saw, so why is there a change in focal
length at all?

**For a bare lens there would not be.** A star at infinity is in focus when the sensor sits at
the focal plane, so the lens-to-sensor distance *is* the focal length, and refocusing always
returns it there. The plate scale of a bare lens cannot be changed by refocusing.

**This is not a bare lens.** It is an NP101 (640 mm) with a reducer onto an ASI6200MM, giving
206265 × 3.76 µm / 1.8474 = **420 mm** effective — a reduction factor of 0.656. With a reducer
in the path the system's effective focal length depends on the **reducer-to-sensor spacing**,
which is exactly what a focuser changes. Focus is one criterion and it fixes sharpness;
magnification is a second degree of freedom and focusing does not pin it. For a reducer of a
few hundred millimetres' focal length the scale moves of order 5 × 10⁻³ per millimetre of
sensor travel, so the measured **−594 ppm needs only about a tenth of a millimetre**. The
change is expected, not anomalous.

What forced the refocus is a separate question and is not settled here. The aluminium tube,
0.72 m and α = 23.1 × 10⁻⁶/K, grows 84 µm per 5 K between a pre-dawn zenith session and
midday; the objective's own focal length drifts a few ppm per kelvin. Both are the right size.
What matters for the measurement is not which, but that the eclipse plate scale is a
measurement of that field and cannot be imported from the calibration — which is the whole
reason this cell is Method 2.

The scatter is large but there is a great deal of it, and the deflection column is what it
multiplies: mean (R☉/r)² = 0.039 over the science set. Nearly all of the inflation from 0.051
to 0.095 is the plate scale — the S–L correlation is **−0.82** — and the rest is the four
observations of a star not being four independent measurements. A useful cross-check: the four
blocks fitted separately give 1.975, 1.721, 1.781, 1.818 ″, a spread of 0.109 ″ whose mean
would have a standard error of 0.054 ″ if the blocks were independent. They are not — they
share their stars — so the pooled 0.095 ″ sitting between that 0.054 and a single block's
0.16 ″ is exactly where it should be.

### Cell 2 against the published analysis (Dittrich et al. 2025, §4.4)

Read and compared line by line. The published analysis of these frames used the 123 × 400 ms
block alone and the same 17 zenith fields.

| | published §4.4 | this reduction |
|---|---|---|
| eclipse data | one block, 123 × 400 ms, 49.2 s | four blocks, 0.25–0.4 s, ~151 s |
| zenith reference | 17 fields, quintic, limiting G 15 | the same 17, quintic, re-centroided windowed |
| stage 2 frees | **the linear terms** (quadratic and above imported) | offsets, rotation and one isotropic scale |
| fit tolerance | **2.0 ″** | 20 ″ then 3 ″ (two-pass) |
| rough threshold | 100 ″ | 100 ″ |
| magnitude limit | G 12 | G 13 (identical in practice) |
| radius | no cut, 2.5–11 R☉ | 2–10 R☉ (see the next section) |
| stars | 171 | 192 stars, 639 observations (165 in the 0.4 s block alone) |
| estimator | 1-D radial, Δθ = L/R + S·R, L and scale together | 2-D vector, per-block offset/rotation/scale, one L |
| **L** | **1.839 ± 0.239 ″** | **1.811 ± 0.084 (stat) ± 0.11 (atm) ″** |
| **plate scale** | **1.847363 ± 0.000013 ″/px** | **1.847368 ± 0.0000055 ″/px** |
| correlation L–scale | **−0.783** | **−0.786** |

**The two analyses agree.** L differs by 0.028 ″, an eighth of the published error. The plate
scales differ by 6 × 10⁻⁶ ″/px, under half the published error, and the correlation between
them comes out the same to within 0.04 — which is a real check, because that correlation is a
property of the star geometry and both analyses had to find it independently.

**Where this reduction gains.** On the *same* 0.4 s block, this chain gives ±0.161 ″ against
the published ±0.239 ″ — a factor 1.4, from the coronal-background subtraction, the windowed
centroids and fitting both components of the displacement rather than the radial projection
alone. Pooling the other three blocks takes it to ±0.084 ″. That is the whole of the
improvement: 2.8× on the statistical error, at an L that has not moved.

**Two things the paper got right that this record had wrong about it.**

*The published analysis did not have the match-gate problem.* §"The two-pass match" describes a
20 ″ fit tolerance forced by an imported plate scale — that is a property of the *constant-only*
mode this record had been using, not of the published work, which freed the **linear** terms
and could therefore run at a **2.0 ″** tolerance. The 100 ″ rough threshold is common to both.
So the two-pass fix does not improve on the published analysis; it brings this record back to
parity with it by a different route (an isotropic scale plus a coarse-then-fine gate rather
than the full linear block). The pipeline flaw is real — `constant` mode cannot fit a scale —
but the published analysis had stepped around it and this record had walked into it.

*The close-in star behaves here.* The paper names Gaia 2577063532462714368, G 9.7, at
1.97 R☉, found only in the 250 ms data, with "the deflection error around 1 arcsec more than
would be expected", and concludes that a single close-in outlier throws off the whole
calculation. It is in this reduction, at **2.02–2.03 R☉ in the 0.25 s and the second 0.3 s
block, with residuals of 0.28 ″ and 0.30 ″** — ordinary. Removing it moves L from 1.823 to
1.788, **+0.035 ″**, a third of the statistical error. The corona subtraction is the likely
reason it is now measurable: it is the same star, at the same radius, in the same frames.
(This is also the star an earlier entry in this record called a 15.9 ″ mis-match at 1.87 R☉;
that was the pre-corona reduction and it was wrong about the radius as well as the match.)

**One thing the paper raises that this reduction does not settle.** §4.4 notes that its 1.839,
Bruns' eclipse-field-only 1.86 and Mikhailov's reanalysis of Lick 1922 (1.83) cluster within
0.03 ″ and "may suggest a systematic error in this method of data analysis" — the method being
a scale-free fit. **This reduction is also scale-free and also lands high: 1.811, 3.4 % above
GR**, where cell 1's imported-scale reduction gives 1.720 and cell 3's 1.914. Our own scale-free
zenith nulls return L = −0.05 ± 0.11 ″, so the estimator shows no bias on fields with no Sun in
them; that is not the same as showing none on an eclipse field. **The test that would settle it
is a matrix-wide one: refit cells 1 and 3 scale-free and see whether they too move up by ~5 %.**
Cell 1 has a bracketed calibration and cell 3 a one-sided one, so both can be run either way.
That is the next thing worth doing on this matrix, and it is now the only open question in
cell 2 that could move the answer.

### The pile-up at 9 R☉ was a cut, and it was in the wrong place (Douglas, 2026-09-04)

Douglas saw points banking up at R = 9 on the deflection charts and asked whether a radius cut
was being applied and what happens if the field is used to its edge. Both fair: 9 R☉ was the
science-set limit, inherited from cells 1 and 3, whose fields are smaller and for whom it never
bound. Station 1's frame reaches **10.6 R☉**, so the cut was discarding 103 observations of
31 stars — and those are the stars that pin the plate scale, which is what inflates σ_L.

| outer cut | observations | stars | L | ± (bootstrap) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9.0 R☉ (the old convention) | 583 | 175 | 1.823 | 0.095 ″ |
| 9.5 | 615 | 184 | 1.823 | 0.091 ″ |
| **10.0 (adopted)** | **639** | **192** | **1.811** | **0.084 ″** |
| the frame edge, 10.6 | 681 | 204 | **1.667** | 0.086 ″ |

Going to the edge moves L by −0.156 ″ and makes σ *worse*. That had to be understood before
any cut could be chosen, and it must not be chosen by looking at L.

**The diagnosis, from the zenith fields.** Comparing the mean *radial* residual annulus by
annulus, eclipse against the sixteen zenith nulls — same optics, same seventeen-field transfer,
offsets, rotation and scale removed in both, no Sun in the zenith case:

| annulus | zenith nulls | eclipse blocks | circle inside the frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2–4 R☉ | −0.021 ± 0.001 ″ | −0.022 ± 0.022 ″ | 100 % |
| 4–6 | +0.014 ± 0.001 | +0.020 ± 0.014 | 100 % |
| 6–8 | −0.005 ± 0.002 | −0.006 ± 0.015 | ~70 % |
| 8–9 | −0.020 ± 0.003 | −0.090 ± 0.023 | 57 → 36 % |
| 9–10 | −0.036 ± 0.004 | −0.040 ± 0.025 | 36 → 24 % |
| **10–10.6** | **+0.043 ± 0.006** | **+0.201 ± 0.024** | **24 → 13 %** |

Out to 10 R☉ the eclipse field behaves like its calibration. In the last half-radius it does
not: **+0.201 ″ outward on eclipse day against +0.043 ″ at the zenith**, a six-sigma excess over
what the instrument does on a night with no Sun. Those stars also sit in the extreme corners —
a circle at 10.5 R☉ is only 13 % inside the sensor — so the bias samples two or three corners
and cannot average over azimuth. Fed to a fit that trades scale against deflection at
ρ = −0.79, an uncancelled outward push at large R is absorbed as scale and pulls L down; hence
the 1.667.

**So the cut moves to 10.0 R☉**, chosen on the zenith-against-eclipse comparison rather than on
L, and the record gains 15 % of its statistical error for a −0.012 ″ shift. What the outermost
annulus is doing is not identified — the coronal-background model is least reliable at the frame
border, where its blur kernel runs off the edge, and that is the first thing to test — but it
does not need to be identified to be excluded, because the zenith fields show the instrument
does not do it.

> **Cell 2, the reduction of record: L = 1.811 ± 0.084 (stat) ± 0.11 (atmosphere) ″, total
> ±0.138.** G ≤ 13, **2–10 R☉**, 639 observations of 192 stars, pooled Method 2 over every
> observation, two-pass stage 2, quintic reference, corona-subtracted, dark + flat. **GR at
> 0.43 σ; Newton excluded at 6.8 σ; L/L_Newton = 2.07 ± 0.16.** Plate scale
> 1.847368 ± 5.5 × 10⁻⁶ ″/px, correlation with L **−0.79**.

And that correlation is worth a second look: Dittrich et al. 2025 report **−0.783** for the same
quantity on the 400 ms block. Two analyses, different estimators, different star sets, the same
number to three decimals — it is a property of the field geometry and both had to find it.

### What differs from the published 2024 analysis, stage by stage (Douglas' question, 2026-09-04)

Douglas' summary, checked against the 2024 archives (`D:\MEE2024 output\Station 1\`, the
stage-1 `results.txt` and stage-2 `distortion_results.txt` of each stack) and against §4.4 of
Dittrich et al. 2025. Right: the same zenith stacks; darks and flats on the eclipse field as
before; four tiers instead of one; an atmosphere term from the zenith fields. Incomplete: the
centroiding changed on *both* sides, and stages 2 and 3 changed more than the stacking did.

Three of these do the work. **The centroid convention** on the eclipse field (moments carry
a magnitude-dependent radial bias on this optic that windowed does not). **The corona
treatment** (which is why the close-in star and the shorter tiers are usable at all). And
**pooling four tiers in two dimensions** (which is where the factor 2.8 on σ comes from:
1.4 from the 2-D fit on the same block, 2 from the other three blocks). Everything else —
the gate, the radius, the magnitude, the vet, the calibration frames — was measured and moves
L by less than 0.04 ″.

Two things stay deliberately as they were, on Douglas' decisions: the quintic, and darks and
flats on the eclipse field with nothing on the zenith fields, so that the comparison above is
about the reduction and not about the frames.
